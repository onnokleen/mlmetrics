14 Advanced Hyperparameter Optimization

14.1 Overview

Hyperparameter optimization (HPO) is the problem of choosing settings that control how a model is estimated. Unlike fitted parameters, these settings are not obtained directly by minimizing the model’s training loss. Examples include tree depth and minimum leaf size, the number of trees in a forest, the learning rate in boosting, or the batch size and network width in a neural network.

For econometricians, HPO matters for two reasons. First, predictive performance can change materially across plausible settings. Second, the act of searching itself creates a statistical problem: if we try many specifications and keep the one with the lowest validation loss, we are optimizing a noisy estimate of out-of-sample performance. Without a disciplined workflow, the selected model can look much better in validation than it will look in truly new data.

This chapter extends the basic validation ideas from the Cross Validation chapter, especially the discussions of Reliable Workflow: Validation vs Test Set, Leakage Inside Cross-Validation, K-Fold Cross-Validation, and Time Series Cross-Validation. The goal here is not to repeat that chapter in full, but to show how those ideas change once hyperparameters themselves become the optimization target. A worked credit-default application later in the chapter makes Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization concrete and compares it with random search under the same evaluation budget.

Definition: Parameters vs Hyperparameters

Parameters are fitted from the training loss once the model class is fixed, such as regression coefficients or tree leaf values.

Hyperparameters are researcher-chosen settings that govern model complexity, regularization, optimization, or resampling design, such as a penalty parameter, tree depth, learning rate, number of trees, or window length.

14.3 Why Reliable HPO Is Hard

Fix a loss function and a training sample, and consider a hyperparameter configuration \lambda, meaning one value for each hyperparameter. Recall from the Cross Validation chapter that the generalization risk of a fitted model is its expected loss on a fresh observation that was not used for fitting, such as a future outcome in a forecasting application. We write c(\lambda) for the generalization risk of the model fitted with configuration \lambda on the training sample. This risk is a fixed but unknown number: the fitted model is held fixed, as in that chapter, and the expectation is taken under the unknown distribution of the fresh observation. We observe only a noisy estimate of c(\lambda) from a validation split, cross-validation scheme, or rolling forecast exercise. If we evaluate many configurations and pick the one with the lowest estimate, the winner is selected partly because of genuine quality and partly because of favorable noise in the estimate.

The clean separation between training, validation, and test data from Reliable Workflow: Validation vs Test Set therefore becomes even more important in HPO. Training data estimate model parameters. Validation data compare hyperparameter settings. Test data remain untouched until the search procedure is finished.

The danger is easy to miss. Even a small comparison can favor a configuration whose validation noise happened to be favorable; a larger search creates more opportunities for this to occur. Once the same validation design is used to compare settings, its validation loss becomes an optimization target. The minimum validation loss across many trials is then usually optimistic. This is the hyperparameter version of specification search (Cawley and Talbot 2010; Varma and Simon 2006).

Selection Bias from Search

For a fixed collection of configurations, suppose configuration j has generalization risk c_j and validation estimate

\hat c_j = c_j + \varepsilon_j, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_j] = 0.

Even if each \hat c_j is unbiased for c_j when considered separately, the minimum validation loss

\min_{1 \le j \le J} \hat c_j

is typically optimistically biased as an estimate of the generalization risk of the selected configuration. Formally,

\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{1 \le j \le J} \hat c_j\right] \le \min_{1 \le j \le J} \mathbb{E}[\hat c_j] = \min_{1 \le j \le J} c_j.

Equality requires at least one fixed configuration to attain the minimum almost surely, possibly tied with others; otherwise the inequality is strict.

This display compares the winning validation loss with the smallest generalization risk in the grid. The claim we actually care about concerns the configuration the search selects, \hat\jmath = \arg\min_j \hat c_j, whose generalization risk c_{\hat\jmath} is itself random because the winner is random. The bridge is one further inequality: since c_{\hat\jmath} \ge \min_j c_j by definition of a minimum, taking expectations gives \mathbb{E}[c_{\hat\jmath}] \ge \min_j c_j. Chaining the two,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\min_{1\le j\le J}\hat c_j\Big] \;\le\; \min_{1\le j\le J} c_j \;\le\; \mathbb{E}\big[c_{\hat\jmath}\big],

The quantity inside the expectation on the left is the reported validation loss \hat c_{\hat\jmath}. Thus the expected reported validation loss is no larger than the expected generalization risk of the selected configuration. This is an average over repeated samples and search outcomes; an individual realized validation loss need not understate its configuration’s risk.

The same logic is visible in the stylized Figure 14.1. The selected configuration is the one with the lowest realized validation loss, but its blue point, which represents its generalization risk, sits above the red validation point.

Recall the nested-validation design: the inner loop selects hyperparameters, and untouched outer blocks evaluate the full procedure “fit, tune, select, refit.” The search procedure includes its search space, algorithm, and stopping budget. Comparing several such procedures on the same outer results would turn those results into another selection criterion. A final assessment should therefore evaluate the procedure chosen before inspecting that assessment sample.

The inner loop must respect the same validation rules as the evaluation of a single model: time-aware splits for time series, and every data-dependent preprocessing step fitted on each inner training sample only. Violating these rules does more damage in HPO than in the evaluation of one fixed model, because the resulting optimism is typically not the same for every configuration. For example, under random K-fold splits of strongly dependent observations, each validation observation has close neighbors in the training sample. Configurations with deep trees or small minimum leaf sizes can fit the local pattern that these neighbors share with the validation observation, whereas heavily regularized configurations exploit it less. The leak therefore lowers the validation loss most for the most flexible configurations and tilts the search toward them.

Question for Reflection

Why is it not enough to say “I never touched the test set” if the hyperparameters were tuned using random K-fold cross-validation on time-series data?

Suggested Answer

Because the problem is not only test-set contamination. Random K-fold cross-validation can already leak future information into the validation loss, so the search is optimized against an invalid target. Even if the final test set is untouched, the chosen hyperparameters may have been selected by exploiting temporal dependence or overlapping information that would not be available in real forecasting use.

14.4 HPO as an Optimization Problem

Write the hyperparameter vector as \lambda \in \Lambda, where \Lambda is the search space. For example, \lambda might contain tree depth, learning rate, minimum leaf size, and a regularization penalty. The ideal target is

\lambda^\star = \arg\min_{\lambda \in \Lambda} c(\lambda),

where c(\lambda) is the generalization risk of configuration \lambda from the previous section. In practice we do not observe c(\lambda). We work with an estimate

\hat c(\lambda),

constructed from a validation split, cross-validation average, rolling-origin loss, or another resampling design.

This formal view clarifies why HPO is not ordinary gradient-based optimization like the parameter fitting studied in Optimization for Machine Learning. The map \lambda \mapsto \hat c(\lambda) is often noisy, non-convex, partly discrete, and sometimes undefined outside admissible regions. Hyperparameters can be integers, categories, or conditional choices. Changing one tuning parameter can change the whole fitted model class, so differentiability is usually unavailable or unhelpful.

A practical HPO problem therefore has four ingredients:

  1. a search space \Lambda
  2. an evaluation rule that produces \hat c(\lambda)
  3. a search algorithm for proposing new \lambda
  4. a compute budget limiting how many evaluations we can afford

If the preferred performance measure is larger-is-better, such as R^2 or a utility proxy, we can either maximize that measure directly or minimize its negative. The statistical issues are the same.

Search-space design matters. A search space that is too narrow cannot recover good configurations. A search space that is too wide wastes budget on implausible values. In econometric applications, a useful starting point is a theory-informed box: plausible lag lengths, sensible regularization ranges, and model sizes that fit the available sample size.

Practical Search-Space Conventions

When building a search space, a few conventions are usually sensible.

  1. Tune scale parameters such as learning rates and penalty strengths on a log scale, because moving from 10^{-4} to 10^{-3} is usually more consequential than moving from 0.100 to 0.101.
  2. Keep structural hyperparameters such as lag length, tree depth, or number of hidden units discrete and bounded by the sample size and the forecasting task.
  3. Use conditional spaces when appropriate. For example, if the search chooses whether a network has a second hidden layer, the width of that layer is relevant only when the layer is present.
  4. Exclude values that are statistically implausible before the search begins. A good search space reflects prior econometric judgment rather than pretending every software-permitted setting is equally credible.

The search algorithm can only evaluate configurations allowed by the search space. Excluding a useful lag length or regularization range cannot be repaired by a more sophisticated acquisition rule, so check these choices before changing algorithms.

14.5 Baseline Search Methods

Grid search. Grid search fixes a finite lattice of candidate values for each hyperparameter and evaluates all combinations. It is simple and reproducible, and it can work well when there are only one or two genuinely important continuous hyperparameters. Its weakness is geometric: if there are many dimensions, the number of combinations grows quickly, and most grid points are spent refining dimensions that may barely matter.

Random search. Random search samples configurations from pre-specified distributions over the search space. It looks naive, but it is a strong baseline because it spends trials on distinct combinations rather than exhaustively refining the same coordinates. When only a small subset of hyperparameters drives performance, random search typically covers the important directions more efficiently than a grid.

This low-effective-dimensionality logic is especially relevant in machine learning applications and is one of the central arguments of Bergstra and Bengio (2012). A researcher may tune ten settings, but perhaps only learning rate, tree depth, and regularization strength move performance materially. A grid wastes effort spreading points evenly in all ten dimensions. Random search has a better chance of finding useful combinations along the few dimensions that matter.

This geometric point is illustrated in Figure 14.2. Both panels use the same number of evaluations, but the random design generates more distinct values along the important horizontal direction. The interpretation is not that random search is always better, but that equal-budget grid search can waste many trials refining irrelevant coordinates.

When baseline methods are enough. If model evaluations are cheap, the search space is modest, and parallel compute is available, random search is often a sensible stopping point. Its trials are easy to parallelize, and its proposals do not depend on fitting a potentially unstable surrogate to a small collection of noisy evaluations.

14.6 Bayesian Optimization

Learning from completed trials. Random search does not use one configuration’s validation loss to choose the next configuration. Bayesian optimization (BO) does: it fits a surrogate model, a statistical model of the relationship between hyperparameter configurations and their losses, and uses that model to decide which configuration to evaluate next. There are therefore two fitted models. The forecasting or classification model maps economic predictors to outcomes; the surrogate maps hyperparameter settings to validation losses. Only evaluating the former requires a new training and validation run (Snoek, Larochelle, and Adams 2012).

We first fix the data splits, preprocessing, and fitting seed so that repeating a configuration gives the same validation loss \hat c(\lambda). The unknown object for the search is this fixed validation surface at configurations we have not tried. The surface remains a sample-based estimate of the generalization risk c(\lambda); learning it precisely does not remove validation-selection bias.

A Gaussian process over the validation surface. A Gaussian process (GP) assigns a joint Gaussian prior to the function values at any finite collection of configurations. Write C(\lambda) for the surrogate’s random function value at \lambda: in this fixed-objective setup, it represents uncertainty about the unobserved value of \hat c(\lambda). For a simple GP, specify a constant prior mean \mu and a covariance kernel k(\lambda,\lambda'), which gives the prior covariance between the function values at two settings. The Gaussian assumption concerns these unknown function values; it does not require the economic predictors or outcomes to be Gaussian.

For one continuous hyperparameter, a concrete choice is the squared-exponential kernel

k(\lambda,\lambda') =\sigma_0^2\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\lambda-\lambda')^2}{2\rho^2}\right], \qquad \sigma_0>0,\quad \rho>0.

Here \sigma_0 is the prior standard deviation of a function value, and the length scale \rho controls how far information travels along the hyperparameter axis. Settings separated by \rho have prior correlation \exp(-1/2)\approx0.61. A larger \rho makes the model relate more distant settings strongly. With several coordinates, the exponent can sum squared distances scaled by a separate length scale for each coordinate. Log-transforming positive scale parameters and mapping the search ranges to [0,1] makes these distances interpretable.

From completed trials to predictions. After t evaluations, the archive is

\mathcal{D}_t=\{(\lambda_j,\hat c_j)\}_{j=1}^t, \qquad \hat c_j=\hat c(\lambda_j).

The index t counts search trials, not calendar dates. Collect the observed losses in the t-vector \hat{\mathbf c}_t, and write \mathbf 1_t for a t-vector of ones. The t\times t matrix K_t has entries k(\lambda_i,\lambda_j); the t-vector \mathbf k_t(\lambda) has entries k(\lambda_j,\lambda). Thus K_t relates completed trials to each other, while \mathbf k_t(\lambda) relates them to the candidate. If \mathbf C_t=(C(\lambda_1),\ldots,C(\lambda_t))^\top, the joint prior is

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf C_t\\C(\lambda)\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal N\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}\mu\mathbf 1_t\\\mu\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} K_t & \mathbf k_t(\lambda)\\ \mathbf k_t(\lambda)^\top & k(\lambda,\lambda) \end{pmatrix} \right).

Condition on \mathbf C_t=\hat{\mathbf c}_t. For fixed prior parameters and nonsingular K_t, the usual multivariate Gaussian-conditioning formula gives (Rasmussen and Williams 2006, chap. 2)

C(\lambda)\mid\mathcal D_t \sim\mathcal N\bigl(m_t(\lambda),s_t(\lambda)^2\bigr),

with

\begin{aligned} m_t(\lambda) &=\mu+\mathbf k_t(\lambda)^\top K_t^{-1} (\hat{\mathbf c}_t-\mu\mathbf 1_t),\\ s_t(\lambda)^2 &=k(\lambda,\lambda)-\mathbf k_t(\lambda)^\top K_t^{-1}\mathbf k_t(\lambda). \end{aligned}

The posterior mean adjusts the prior level using observed losses and their covariance with the candidate. The posterior variance subtracts the information provided by completed trials from the prior variance. At an evaluated setting, noise-free conditioning gives m_t(\lambda_j)=\hat c_j and s_t(\lambda_j)=0. Far from all evaluated settings under this kernel, the covariances approach zero, so the prediction returns toward the prior mean and variance. The uncertainty band is conditional on the chosen GP model, rather than a confidence interval for the generalization risk.

We hold \mu, \sigma_0, and \rho fixed in the example below to make every step visible. Software can instead estimate GP parameters from the Gaussian likelihood of the archived losses. These are settings of the surrogate, distinct from the model hyperparameters \lambda being searched. In code, solve linear systems involving K_t rather than forming its inverse explicitly.

Choosing the next expensive evaluation. The incumbent is the best evaluated configuration, with loss c_{\min,t}=\min_{1\le j\le t}\hat c_j. An acquisition function scores candidates using the surrogate. For minimization, expected improvement (EI) is

\operatorname{EI}_t(\lambda) =\mathbb E\left[\max\{c_{\min,t}-C(\lambda),0\}\mid\mathcal D_t\right].

Improvement is positive only when the unknown loss beats the incumbent. A candidate with a low predicted loss can improve the incumbent, but so can a less promising candidate whose uncertainty leaves enough probability of a much lower loss. EI therefore balances exploitation, pursuing low predicted losses, and exploration, evaluating uncertain configurations (Jones, Schonlau, and Welch 1998; Snoek, Larochelle, and Adams 2012).

Expected Improvement Under a Gaussian Surrogate

For C(\lambda)\mid\mathcal D_t\sim\mathcal N(m_t(\lambda),s_t(\lambda)^2) and s_t(\lambda)>0,

\operatorname{EI}_t(\lambda) =\bigl(c_{\min,t}-m_t(\lambda)\bigr)\Phi(z_t(\lambda)) +s_t(\lambda)\phi(z_t(\lambda)),

where

z_t(\lambda)=\frac{c_{\min,t}-m_t(\lambda)}{s_t(\lambda)},

and \Phi and \phi denote the standard normal cumulative distribution function (CDF) and probability density function (PDF). If s_t(\lambda)=0, the appropriate limit is

\operatorname{EI}_t(\lambda)=\max\{c_{\min,t}-m_t(\lambda),0\}.

The exercise on GP BO and TPE derives the closed form from the expectation.

GP Bayesian Optimization: The Complete Loop

For a fixed, repeatable validation objective:

  1. Specify the search space, validation rule, and total evaluation budget. Transform and scale the hyperparameter coordinates, and choose a GP mean and kernel.
  2. Evaluate several initial configurations sampled across the search space. Each trial fits the prediction model and records its validation loss in \mathcal D_t.
  3. Condition the GP on the archive to obtain m_t(\lambda) and s_t(\lambda) for unevaluated candidates.
  4. Compute EI and choose \lambda_{t+1}\in\arg\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{EI}_t(\lambda), using a fixed rule to break ties. A grid or candidate pool provides an approximate maximization over a continuous space; optimizing EI requires only surrogate calculations.
  5. Fit and validate the prediction model at \lambda_{t+1}, append its observed loss to the archive, and repeat Steps 3–5 until the evaluation budget is exhausted.
  6. Select the evaluated configuration with the lowest observed validation loss, refit the prediction model on the available development data, and evaluate once on the reserved assessment sample.

The next trial maximizes EI; the final selection minimizes observed validation loss. In the time-series workflow, each expensive evaluation runs the specified inner rolling or expanding folds, and the reserved assessment sample is the outer validation block.

Example: One Complete GP-BO Iteration

Suppose \lambda\in[0,1] is a rescaled log penalty for a ridge forecasting model, with all other choices fixed. Use illustrative validation losses from two completed trials: \hat c(0.25)=0.55 and \hat c(0.75)=0.50. Set \mu=0.60, \sigma_0=0.10, and \rho=0.25. The covariance matrix is

K_2=0.01\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{-2}\\e^{-2}&1\end{pmatrix}.

Search the grid \{0,0.01,\ldots,1\}, excluding completed trials. At \lambda=0.56, the covariance vector is approximately

\mathbf k_2(0.56)\approx\begin{pmatrix}0.004636\\0.007492\end{pmatrix}.

Substitute the observed deviations from the prior mean into the conditioning formula:

\begin{aligned} m_2(0.56) &=0.60+\mathbf k_2(0.56)^\top K_2^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}-0.05\\-0.10\end{pmatrix}\\ &\approx0.5116,\\ s_2(0.56) &=\sqrt{0.01-\mathbf k_2(0.56)^\top K_2^{-1}\mathbf k_2(0.56)}\\ &\approx0.0552. \end{aligned}

Using the unrounded predictions and c_{\min,2}=0.50, we obtain z_2(0.56)\approx-0.2105 and \operatorname{EI}_2(0.56)\approx0.0167, the largest EI on the grid. For comparison:

Candidate \lambda Posterior mean Posterior standard deviation EI
0.00 0.5764 0.0792 0.0070
0.50 0.5199 0.0593 0.0150
0.56 0.5116 0.0552 0.0167
1.00 0.5420 0.0792 0.0149

Only now do we fit and validate the ridge model at 0.56. Suppose the observed loss is 0.46. Append (0.56,0.46) to \mathcal D_2, update the incumbent to 0.46, and condition the GP on all three trials. The new GP interpolates 0.46 at 0.56, where its posterior standard deviation and EI are zero. Its predictions and EI elsewhere also change, determining the following trial.

Show the code 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import norm

prior_mean = 0.60
prior_std = 0.10
length_scale = 0.25

def gp_covariance(left, right):
    distance = np.subtract.outer(left, right) / length_scale
    return prior_std**2 * np.exp(-0.5 * distance**2)

def condition_gp(trial_points, trial_losses, candidates):
    covariance = gp_covariance(trial_points, trial_points)
    cross_covariance = gp_covariance(trial_points, candidates)
    mean = prior_mean + cross_covariance.T @ np.linalg.solve(
        covariance, trial_losses - prior_mean,
    )
    variance = prior_std**2 - np.sum(
        cross_covariance * np.linalg.solve(covariance, cross_covariance),
        axis=0,
    )
    return mean, np.sqrt(np.maximum(variance, 0.0))

def gaussian_ei(mean, std, incumbent):
    improvement = incumbent - mean
    positive_std = std > 1e-12
    z = np.divide(
        improvement, std, out=np.zeros_like(mean), where=positive_std,
    )
    return np.where(
        positive_std,
        improvement * norm.cdf(z) + std * norm.pdf(z),
        np.maximum(improvement, 0.0),
    )

trial_points = np.array([0.25, 0.75])
trial_losses = np.array([0.55, 0.50])
candidates = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 101)
mean_before, std_before = condition_gp(trial_points, trial_losses, candidates)
ei_before = gaussian_ei(mean_before, std_before, trial_losses.min())
evaluated = np.isclose(candidates[:, None], trial_points).any(axis=1)
ei_before[evaluated] = 0.0
next_index = int(np.argmax(ei_before))  # first grid point wins a tie
next_point = candidates[next_index]
new_loss = 0.46  # stipulated outcome of the new, expensive validation run

updated_points = np.append(trial_points, next_point)
updated_losses = np.append(trial_losses, new_loss)
mean_after, std_after = condition_gp(updated_points, updated_losses, candidates)
ei_after = gaussian_ei(mean_after, std_after, updated_losses.min())
evaluated_after = np.isclose(candidates[:, None], updated_points).any(axis=1)
ei_after[evaluated_after] = 0.0

fig, axes = plt.subplots(
    2, 2, figsize=(11.5, 6.8), sharex=True, sharey="row",
    gridspec_kw={"height_ratios": [2.2, 1]},
)
for column, (mean, std, ei, incumbent, title) in enumerate([
    (mean_before, std_before, ei_before, trial_losses.min(), "Before: two completed trials"),
    (mean_after, std_after, ei_after, updated_losses.min(), "After: three completed trials"),
]):
    upper, lower = axes[:, column]
    upper.plot(candidates, mean, color="C0", linewidth=2, label="GP mean")
    upper.fill_between(
        candidates, mean - 1.96 * std, mean + 1.96 * std,
        color="C0", alpha=0.18, label="95% posterior band",
    )
    upper.scatter(trial_points, trial_losses, color="black", s=45, zorder=4,
                  label="Initial trials")
    upper.axhline(incumbent, color="C3", linestyle="--", label="Incumbent loss")
    upper.set_title(title)
    lower.plot(candidates, ei, color="C2", linewidth=2)
    lower.fill_between(candidates, 0, ei, color="C2", alpha=0.18)
    lower.set_xlabel("Rescaled log penalty")
    for ax in (upper, lower):
        ax.axvline(next_point, color="0.4", linestyle=":", linewidth=1.4)

Question for Reflection

A GP is fitted to 12 completed trials and predicts losses at 2,500 unevaluated candidate configurations. How many new fits of the forecasting model are needed to compute all 2,500 EI values? What must happen before the archive can contain a thirteenth trial?

Suggested Answer

Computing the EI values requires no new forecasting-model fits; the GP uses the existing 12 configuration–loss pairs. After selecting a candidate, run the actual training and validation procedure there and append the observed loss. If validation uses several folds, that one trial requires a model fit within each fold. A surrogate prediction cannot substitute for the observed validation result.

What changes with noisy evaluations? The preceding loop models a fixed validation surface. If repeated runs vary, or if we instead model the latent generalization risk c(\lambda) using sample validation losses, an observation model is needed. In the latter formulation, reinterpret C(\lambda) as the surrogate’s random belief about c(\lambda) and write \hat c_j=c(\lambda_j)+\varepsilon_j. Suppose the error vector is Gaussian with mean zero and covariance \Sigma_t, independent of the latent GP. Gaussian conditioning then replaces K_t^{-1} in both prediction formulas by (K_t+\Sigma_t)^{-1}. The resulting s_t(\lambda) describes uncertainty about the latent function; it excludes the extra noise of a fresh evaluation. Shared validation observations can create correlated errors, so taking \Sigma_t diagonal is a working approximation rather than a consequence of cross-validation.

With noisy observations, \min_j\hat c_j can select a favorable noise realization. One practical plug-in benchmark is

c_{\min,t}=\min_{1\le j\le t}m_t(\lambda_j),

the smallest posterior mean at the evaluated points. The same EI formula applies because this benchmark is fixed conditional on the archive, although it does not integrate uncertainty about the incumbent itself. The appropriate final selection also needs to account for noise, for example by checking promising configurations across several training seeds when training randomness is the concern. Such repetitions do not remove the sampling uncertainty from reusing the same validation observations.

GP BO is most attractive when each evaluation is expensive and the search dimension is modest. In larger spaces, a small archive provides less information about nearby configurations; categorical and conditional choices require additional kernel design; and weakly informative, noisy losses can make the surrogate unreliable. Sequentially choosing trials also limits the immediate parallelism available to plain random search. The worked credit-default application uses the simpler fixed-objective setup developed above.

Tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE). TPE is a second Bayesian-optimization method: it also learns from completed trials where to search next, but it replaces the GP surrogate. Instead of modeling the loss as a function of \lambda directly, TPE splits past trials into a good set and a bad set using a loss threshold c^\ast. For this construction, write \widehat C for a trial’s random observed validation loss and c for its realized value. In the noisy GP formulation above, C(\lambda) models latent risk; \widehat C instead includes the evaluation noise of the validation loss. In an idealized continuous setting without ties, c^\ast can be chosen as the \gamma-quantile so that a fraction \gamma of past trials falls into the good set. With a finite archive and tied losses, an implementation also needs a deterministic rank or tie-breaking rule. TPE then fits two regularized densities. In the original algorithm both are kernel density estimates, for which Parzen estimator is an older name (Bergstra et al. 2011); the kernel smoothing primer introduces the basic idea. The two densities are

\ell(\lambda) = p(\lambda \mid \widehat C \le c^\ast), \qquad g(\lambda) = p(\lambda \mid \widehat C > c^\ast).

The search idea is simple: propose candidates that look likely under the good-trial density and unlikely under the bad-trial density. Where both fitted densities are positive, this means favoring large values of

\frac{\ell(\lambda)}{g(\lambda)}.

In the original TPE paper (Bergstra et al. 2011), this density-ratio criterion is linked directly to expected improvement under the TPE construction.

TPE is appealing because it handles mixed and conditional search spaces more easily than a GP surrogate. It also avoids forcing a smooth global regression model onto a search space that may include integers, categories, and hard constraints.

TPE Algorithm

One practical TPE workflow can be summarized as follows.

  1. Evaluate an initial set of randomly sampled hyperparameter configurations.
  2. Split the evaluated archive into a good set and a bad set using a \gamma-quantile threshold on the observed validation losses, with an explicit rule for ties.
  3. Fit regularized densities \ell(\lambda)=p(\lambda\mid \widehat C \le c^\ast) and g(\lambda)=p(\lambda\mid \widehat C > c^\ast) so that candidate ratios remain well-defined.
  4. Sample candidate configurations from the good density and rank them by the ratio \ell(\lambda)/g(\lambda).
  5. Evaluate the candidate with the largest ratio, update the archive, and repeat until the budget is exhausted.

The tuning parameter \gamma controls how selective the “good” set is. Smaller \gamma focuses more aggressively on the currently best-performing trials.

Figure 14.4 displays the fitted good- and bad-trial curves and their ratio over a one-dimensional range. Compare the location of the good-trial mode with the marked ratio maximum: the two need not coincide.

The marked point is near the left boundary, where both curves are small. The bad-trial density is even smaller than the good-trial density there, so their ratio is large. TPE uses this relative comparison, not the good-trial density alone. The diagram maximizes over a displayed grid; the algorithm above instead selects among candidates sampled from \ell, so a low-probability region may never enter a finite candidate set.

GP BO versus TPE. Both are sequential model-based search methods. A GP expresses smoothness assumptions directly in a modest continuous space. TPE can specify separate densities for categorical choices and their active conditional hyperparameters, which makes those search spaces easier to represent. These modeling differences do not establish a universal ranking by validation performance; their usefulness depends on the search space, evaluation noise, and trial budget.

Question for Reflection

Consider two settings. In (A), there are four continuous hyperparameters, 25 trials are allowed, each validation run takes 2 hours, and the validation objective is moderately noisy but reasonably smooth. In (B), there are twelve mixed and conditional hyperparameters, 500 trials are allowed, each run takes 10 seconds, and there is no strong reason to expect a smooth low-dimensional objective. In which setting is Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization more likely to improve on random search, and why is random search more defensible in the other?

Suggested Answer

Gaussian-process BO is more likely to help in setting A. Each avoided evaluation saves substantial time, and the modest continuous space plus assumed smoothness gives the surrogate a plausible structure to learn. In setting B, 500 draws do not densely cover a twelve-dimensional space, but they do provide broad, easily parallelized exploration at low cost. Mixed and conditional coordinates make a standard GP surrogate harder to specify, and its sequential overhead is harder to justify when each trial takes only seconds. A specialized surrogate such as TPE could still exploit the conditional structure, so this comparison is about standard GP BO rather than all model-based search.

14.7 Worked Application: Bayesian HPO for Credit Default

The preceding GP-BO algorithm describes how completed trials determine the next evaluation. We now apply that rule to the Taiwan credit-card default data, which the gradient-boosting chapter used for a common comparison of logistic regression, neural networks, and tree-based methods. The outcome indicates whether a client defaults in the month after the observed repayment histories. The present application changes the object of interest: instead of constructing another broad model ranking, we study how two search procedures allocate the same limited number of validation evaluations.

We use the same stratified 60% training, 20% validation, and 20% test split as the earlier chapters. The randomized split is appropriate only for this within-cohort cross-sectional illustration under an exchangeability approximation; it is not an out-of-time credit-risk design. The test clients have already appeared in the earlier model comparison, so the test columns below are descriptive follow-up results rather than fresh independent evidence.

Search space and budget. Every trial fits a gradient-boosting classifier with 100 depth-2 trees. Hyperparameter optimization varies two quantities:

  • the learning rate over [0.01,0.20] on a logarithmic scale
  • the minimum number of training observations in a leaf over [10,400] on a logarithmic scale, rounded to an integer

The two logarithmic coordinates are mapped to the unit square before fitting the GP surrogate. This modest two-dimensional space is a favorable setting for GP Bayesian optimization: both coordinates are ordered, evaluations are costly enough to ration, and the surrogate can represent nearby configurations as related. Rounding the leaf-size coordinate introduces a small discrete component, so the objective is not literally a smooth function on the entire square.

Both methods receive 15 model evaluations and share the same first five randomly drawn configurations. Random search draws its remaining ten configurations independently. Bayesian optimization instead fits a Matérn-kernel GP after every completed trial and selects the next point by maximizing expected improvement over a fixed pool of 2,500 candidate points. Predicting losses and computing EI for that pool uses only the surrogate; each selected point then requires one new classifier fit and validation evaluation. Validation log loss is the only search objective. Preprocessing is estimated on the training sample and applied unchanged to validation observations.

Here the split, preprocessing, and classifier seed are fixed. The GP therefore models the deterministic validation surface \lambda\mapsto\hat c(\lambda) on this sample, using the same EI calculation with that surface as its latent function. Its uncertainty concerns configurations not yet evaluated; it is not a confidence interval for the generalization risk c(\lambda). We use a Matérn covariance kernel, an alternative to the squared-exponential kernel with a parameter controlling function smoothness. The code fixes its smoothness parameter at 2.5 and its length scale at 0.3 in each unit-square coordinate, and standardizes the observed losses before fitting the GP. A small training-diagonal regularizer provides numerical stability. The regularizer is fixed in standardized-loss units and is not an estimate of validation-sampling variance. The standard deviation supplied to EI is the latent-function standard deviation.

14.8 Multi-Fidelity Search

When each model can be evaluated at different resource levels, full-budget tuning may be wasteful. The resource might be training epochs, sample size, number of boosting rounds, or another quantity that lets us obtain a cheap but noisy preview of eventual performance.

Successive halving. Start with many configurations at a small resource level r. Rank them by validation performance. Keep only the top fraction, typically 1/\eta, and evaluate the survivors at total resource \eta r. Repeat until only a small set of configurations is left at a high resource level.

This logic can save large amounts of computation because poorly performing configurations are discarded early. The key assumption is that early, cheap performance is informative about later, expensive performance.

Successive Halving Algorithm

Let p^{[0]} be a positive integer giving the initial number of configurations, r^{[0]}>0 the initial resource per configuration, \eta>1 the elimination rate, and T_{\mathrm{SH}} the terminal round index. Choose resource levels that are feasible for the training procedure and specify a fixed rule for breaking tied validation losses. Start at round t=0.

  1. At round t, evaluate the current p^{[t]} configurations at total resource r^{[t]} and rank them by validation loss.
  2. If t=T_{\mathrm{SH}}, fewer than \eta configurations remain, or the next rung would exceed the budget, stop and return the lowest-loss configuration evaluated at this rung, using the stated tie rule.
  3. Otherwise retain the best p^{[t+1]} configurations, where p^{[t+1]}=\left\lfloor\frac{p^{[t]}}{\eta}\right\rfloor, \qquad r^{[t+1]}=\eta r^{[t]}.
  4. Advance to round t+1 and repeat. Stopping before another elimination ensures that the final evaluated survivor is retained.

Successive halving is efficient because it trades breadth for depth: many configurations get a cheap initial test, while only promising ones receive expensive training.

The resource accounting depends on implementation. If every rung trains a fresh model from the beginning, round t costs p^{[t]}r^{[t]}. If promoted configurations resume from saved checkpoints, round 0 costs p^{[0]}r^{[0]} and later rounds add only p^{[t]}(r^{[t]}-r^{[t-1]}). We keep these two conventions separate below.

Hyperband. Successive halving depends on the initial tradeoff between number of configurations and initial budget. Hyperband addresses this by running several halving brackets with different starting points. Some brackets try many configurations very cheaply. Others start with fewer configurations but give each one more initial resources. This hedges against the risk of choosing the wrong early-stopping aggressiveness (Li et al. 2018).

Hyperband as a Family of Brackets

Measure resources in units of the minimum admissible allocation, so that the minimum resource is 1. Let R_{\max}\ge1 be the maximum resource per configuration in those units and let \eta>1 be the elimination rate. Define

s_{\max} = \left\lfloor \log_{\eta}(R_{\max}) \right\rfloor, \qquad B = (s_{\max}+1)R_{\max}.

Here B is the nominal resource budget allocated to each bracket, measured in the same units as R_{\max}. Under the from-scratch accounting used in the standard bracket formulas, a bracket spends roughly what it would cost to train s_{\max}+1 configurations to the full resource level. Since Hyperband runs s_{\max}+1 brackets, one full sweep has nominal cost approximately (s_{\max}+1)B.

Hyperband then runs several successive-halving brackets indexed by s=s_{\max},\ldots,0. The bracket index s is distinct from the within-bracket round index t. A common parameterization is

n_s = \left\lceil \frac{B}{R_{\max}}\frac{\eta^s}{s+1} \right\rceil, \qquad r_s = R_{\max}\eta^{-s},

where bracket s starts with n_s configurations at resource level r_s. Large-s brackets explore many configurations cheaply, while small-s brackets explore fewer configurations more deeply from the start.

Figure 14.6 shows how these brackets differ for R_{\max}=27 and \eta=3, so that s_{\max}=3 and B=108. The wide bracket starts with many configurations and a very small resource level, while the deep bracket starts with fewer configurations but allocates more resources immediately. The two features worth checking against the formula are that the starting counts n_s fall as s decreases (27, 12, 6, 4) while the initial resource levels r_s rise (1, 3, 9, 27), and that the nominal from-scratch spend is roughly equal across brackets. This near-equality lets Hyperband hedge across breadth-versus-depth tradeoffs under a common accounting convention.

When multi-fidelity methods work well. Hyperband is most attractive when:

  • full training runs are expensive
  • partial learning curves are informative
  • many hyperparameter settings are clearly poor and can be pruned early

Failure modes. Hyperband can be unreliable when validation losses at low resource levels are noisy, non-monotone, or systematically biased against slow starters. This is common in deep learning, but it can also occur in econometric forecasting when the validation block is short or when instability early in training is not informative about final performance. In those settings, a larger minimum budget or a less aggressive elimination rule may be needed.

14.9 Practical Workflow for Econometricians

The tuning method should match the statistical design, not just the software defaults.

  1. Choose the evaluation metric first. Decide whether the target is mean squared forecast error, log score, continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), classification loss, or another application-specific measure. Hyperparameters should be selected against the criterion that actually matters.
  2. Choose a validation design that respects the information set. For independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data, K-fold cross-validation may be adequate. For temporal dependence, use the expanding windows, rolling windows, or purged time-aware splits of the Cross Validation chapter as appropriate; for clustered or panel observations, also match any group holdout to the intended prediction task.
  3. Define a defensible search space. Use theory, prior empirical knowledge, and sample-size limits to avoid absurd configurations.
  4. Start with a baseline. Random search is a strong default because it is simple, parallel, and robust.
  5. Escalate only when the budget justifies it. Use BO when evaluations are expensive enough that learning from past trials matters. Use Hyperband when partial-resource evaluations are genuinely informative.
  6. Refit only after tuning is complete. Once the hyperparameters are selected, refit the model on the full training sample available before the final test period.
  7. Keep the final test set for one last check. If the final test result disappoints, that is information about the procedure, not an invitation to keep tuning on the same test period.

Packages such as Optuna make these search strategies convenient to run, especially random search, TPE, and pruning-based workflows. The statistical issues do not disappear just because the interface is convenient. Good tooling cannot rescue an invalid validation design.

14.10 Summary

Key Takeaways
  1. Hyperparameter optimization chooses model settings using a noisy estimate of out-of-sample performance.
  2. Random search provides a baseline by sampling from the specified search space.
  3. Bayesian optimization uses completed evaluations to guide the choice of subsequent trials.
  4. Successive halving and Hyperband allocate more computation to configurations that survive cheaper partial evaluations.
Common Pitfalls
  • Repeatedly consulting the test set turns it into another tuning criterion.
  • Fit preprocessing inside each training fold, including when it is itself a tuning choice.
  • Forecast validation must respect time order, publication lags and overlapping target horizons.
  • Aggressive early pruning can discard slow configurations that would perform well with a larger training budget.

Exercise 14.1: Gaussian-Process Bayesian Optimization and TPE

Suppose you are tuning a forecasting model with four hyperparameters: learning_rate, max_depth, min_samples_leaf, and subsample. Each model evaluation requires a full rolling-window validation exercise and is therefore expensive.

  1. Derive the closed form of expected improvement. Under the Gaussian surrogate C(\lambda)\mid\mathcal{D}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(m,s^2) with s>0—writing m=m_t(\lambda), s=s_t(\lambda), and c_{\min}=c_{\min,t} to keep the algebra light—start from the definition \operatorname{EI}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}\big[\max\{c_{\min}-C(\lambda),0\}\mid\mathcal{D}_t\big] and show that \operatorname{EI}(\lambda) = (c_{\min}-m)\Phi(z) + s\,\phi(z), \qquad z=\frac{c_{\min}-m}{s}.
  2. In the closed form from Part 1, identify the term that rewards a low predicted loss and the term that rewards uncertainty. What happens to \operatorname{EI} as s\downarrow 0 with m>c_{\min} held fixed?
  3. In a Gaussian-process BO scheme, the current best observed validation loss is c_{\min}=0.45. The surrogate model predicts:
    • Configuration A: posterior mean m_A=0.42, posterior standard deviation s_A=0.01.
    • Configuration B: posterior mean m_B=0.47, posterior standard deviation s_B=0.10.
    Expected improvement is \mathrm{EI}(\lambda)=s\left[z\,\Phi(z)+\phi(z)\right], \qquad z=\frac{c_{\min}-m}{s}, where \Phi and \phi are the standard normal CDF and PDF. Using \Phi(3)\approx 0.999, \phi(3)\approx 0.004, \Phi(-0.2)\approx 0.421, and \phi(-0.2)\approx 0.391, compute \mathrm{EI} for both configurations and explain which one the acquisition function prefers.
  4. Show why TPE ranks candidates by the density ratio. TPE models the trials as p(\lambda \mid c) = \ell(\lambda) if c \le c^\ast and g(\lambda) if c > c^\ast, with \mathbb{P}(\widehat C \le c^\ast) = \gamma for the random observed validation loss \widehat C. Assume 0<\gamma<1, positive fitted densities at the candidate, and a continuous loss distribution with no mass at c^\ast. Using p(\lambda) = \gamma\,\ell(\lambda) + (1-\gamma)\,g(\lambda) and the definition \operatorname{EI}(\lambda) = \int_{-\infty}^{c^\ast} (c^\ast - c)\, p(c \mid \lambda)\, dc, together with Bayes’ rule p(c\mid\lambda) = p(\lambda\mid c)p(c)/p(\lambda), show that \operatorname{EI}(\lambda) = \frac{A}{\gamma + (1-\gamma)\,g(\lambda)/\ell(\lambda)} for a positive constant A that does not depend on \lambda. Conclude that maximizing \operatorname{EI} is equivalent to maximizing \ell(\lambda)/g(\lambda). As a brief check of your ranking rule, identify the preferred candidate when the three ratios are 1.2, 2.8, and 0.9.
  5. Give two conditions under which Bayesian optimization is likely to outperform plain random search, and one condition under which random search may still be preferable.

Exam-level. Parts 1 and 4 derive the Gaussian closed form and TPE density-ratio criterion. Parts 2 and 3 interpret or apply EI, and Part 5 connects the search method to the evaluation budget and search space.

Hint for Part 1

Standardize first: write C = m + sZ so the expectation becomes an integral against the standard normal density over the region where the improvement is positive. For the harder of the two resulting integrals, use the fact that the standard normal density satisfies \phi'(u) = -u\,\phi(u).

Hint for Part 4

Over the whole region of integration c \le c^\ast, the conditional density p(\lambda\mid c) equals \ell(\lambda) and does not depend on c—so it factors out of the integral, leaving something that does not involve \lambda at all.

Solution

Part 1: The Gaussian closed form

Write C = m + sZ with Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1). Then c_{\min} - C = s(z - Z) with z = (c_{\min}-m)/s, and since s>0,

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{EI} &= \mathbb{E}\big[\max\{s(z-Z),0\}\big] \\ &= s\,\mathbb{E}\big[\max\{z-Z,0\}\big] \\ &= s\int_{-\infty}^{z}(z-u)\phi(u)\,du. \end{aligned}

Split the integral:

\begin{aligned} \int_{-\infty}^{z}(z-u)\phi(u)\,du &= z\int_{-\infty}^{z}\phi(u)\,du - \int_{-\infty}^{z}u\,\phi(u)\,du \\ &= z\Phi(z) - \int_{-\infty}^{z}u\,\phi(u)\,du . \end{aligned}

The remaining integral has a closed form because the standard normal density satisfies \phi'(u) = -u\,\phi(u), so u\,\phi(u) = -\phi'(u) and

\begin{aligned} \int_{-\infty}^{z}u\,\phi(u)\,du &= -\big[\phi(u)\big]_{-\infty}^{z} \\ &= -\phi(z). \end{aligned}

Therefore

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{EI} &= s\big[z\Phi(z) + \phi(z)\big] \\ &= (c_{\min}-m)\Phi(z) + s\,\phi(z), \end{aligned}

using sz = c_{\min}-m in the last step.

Part 2: Exploitation, exploration, and the zero-uncertainty limit

The first term, (c_{\min}-m)\Phi(z), is the exploitation term: it is large when the predicted loss m sits below the incumbent, weighted by the probability of improving. The second term, s\phi(z), is the exploration term: it reflects posterior uncertainty.

As s \downarrow 0 with m > c_{\min} fixed, we have z \to -\infty, so \operatorname{EI} \to 0. A point the surrogate is certain is worse than the incumbent has no expected improvement.

Part 3: EI computation and exploration

For Configuration A: z_A=(0.45-0.42)/0.01=3, so

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{EI}_A &= 0.01\big[3\times\Phi(3)+\phi(3)\big] \\ &= 0.01\big[3\times 0.999+0.004\big] \\ &= 0.01\times 3.001 \\ &\approx 0.030. \end{aligned}

For Configuration B: z_B=(0.45-0.47)/0.10=-0.2, so

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{EI}_B &= 0.10\big[-0.2\times\Phi(-0.2)+\phi(-0.2)\big] \\ &= 0.10\big[-0.2\times 0.421+0.391\big] \\ &= 0.10\times 0.307 \\ &\approx 0.031. \end{aligned}

Configuration B has slightly higher EI despite a worse posterior mean. The high posterior uncertainty creates enough probability of beating c_{\min} that the exploration payoff outweighs A’s lower point prediction. This is why expected improvement naturally balances exploitation and exploration.

Part 4: The TPE density-ratio criterion

Apply Bayes’ rule inside the integral. Since p(c\mid\lambda) = p(\lambda\mid c)p(c)/p(\lambda) and p(\lambda\mid c) = \ell(\lambda) throughout the region c \le c^\ast,

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{EI}(\lambda) &= \frac{1}{p(\lambda)}\int_{-\infty}^{c^\ast}(c^\ast-c)\,\ell(\lambda)\,p(c)\,dc \\ &= \frac{\ell(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{c^\ast}(c^\ast-c)\,p(c)\,dc}_{=:\,A}, \end{aligned}

where A>0 depends only on c^\ast and the marginal distribution of the losses, not on \lambda. Substituting the mixture p(\lambda) = \gamma\ell(\lambda) + (1-\gamma)g(\lambda) and dividing numerator and denominator by the positive \ell(\lambda),

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{EI}(\lambda) &= \frac{A\,\ell(\lambda)}{\gamma\ell(\lambda)+(1-\gamma)g(\lambda)} \\ &= \frac{A}{\gamma + (1-\gamma)\,g(\lambda)/\ell(\lambda)} . \end{aligned}

Because A>0 and \gamma \in (0,1), the right-hand side is strictly increasing in \ell(\lambda)/g(\lambda). Maximizing expected improvement is therefore equivalent to maximizing the density ratio under the stated idealized assumptions.

For the brief numerical check, the ratios are 1.2, 2.8, and 0.9, so TPE evaluates the second candidate next. It is relatively most likely under the density fitted to good trials compared with the density fitted to bad trials.

Part 5: When BO should or should not beat random search

Bayesian optimization is likely to outperform random search when:

  1. each evaluation is expensive, so using past trials to guide future ones is valuable
  2. the search dimension is modest enough that a surrogate can learn something useful from a limited number of observations

Random search may still be preferable when the search space is very high-dimensional, highly discrete, or so noisy that the surrogate learns little. It is also attractive when many evaluations can be run in parallel cheaply.

Exercise 14.2: Hyperband Under Noisy Learning Curves

A neural network predicts future firm default probabilities from panel data. A full training run takes 81 epochs, corresponding to 9 hours of computation per configuration. A successive-halving bracket with elimination rate \eta=3 starts with 27 configurations at 3 epochs. Promoted configurations resume from checkpoints.

To study screening errors separately from the full bracket, consider a two-configuration comparison in which only the smaller early validation loss is promoted. Configuration A has lower full-budget generalization risk than B, with difference \Delta=c_A-c_B=-0.02. At each validation date j, let D_j be A’s average loss across the validation firms minus B’s average loss across the same firms. Use the stylized Gaussian model

D_j=\Delta+b+u_j,

where b is the relative disadvantage of A from evaluating both models before training is complete. The mean-zero errors u_j are jointly Gaussian with variance \tau^2>0. Use n consecutive validation dates and let \bar D_n=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^n D_j. Configuration A is discarded when \bar D_n>0. Under the covariance assumptions in Parts 3 and 4, equality has probability zero.

  1. Give the number of configurations and the total epoch level at each rung. Calculate the bracket’s incremental checkpoint cost and compare it with training all 27 configurations to 81 epochs.
  2. For a general schedule p^{[t]}=p^{[0]}\eta^{-t} and r^{[t]}=r^{[0]}\eta^t, t=0,\ldots,T_{\mathrm{SH}}, ignore rounding and derive total compute under (i) restarting at each rung and (ii) checkpoint continuation. Verify both expressions for the numerical bracket. Explain why equal nominal Hyperband bracket budgets need not imply equal incremental costs.
  3. First assume the u_j are independent. Derive the probability that the better full-budget configuration A is discarded, expressing it using \Phi. Compare its limit as n\to\infty when b=0 and when b=0.03. Does a longer validation window necessarily repair the disadvantage of a slow-starting model?
  4. Now set b=0 and allow serial dependence, with \operatorname{Cov}(u_j,u_{j+h})=\tau^2\rho^h for h\ge1 and 0\le\rho<1. Derive \operatorname{Var}(\bar D_n) by counting pairs at each lag, and hence the discard probability. For fixed n>1, explain how positive correlation changes that probability relative to the independent case. Distinguish what a larger initial training budget addresses from what a longer or more informative validation window addresses.

Exam level. Parts 1 and 2 connect the resource schedule to two accounting conventions. Parts 3 and 4 derive screening-error probabilities and separate slow learning from serially dependent validation noise. Answers may be left in terms of the standard normal CDF; no probability tables or code are required.

Hint for Part 2

In a promoted rung, continuation pays for r^{[t]}-r^{[t-1]} additional epochs per survivor. Substitute the geometric schedules before summing over rungs.

Hint for Part 4

At lag h, there are n-h distinct pairs of validation dates. Each covariance enters the variance of the sum twice.

Solution

Part 1: Schedule and checkpoint cost

The successive rungs are 27 configurations at 3 epochs, 9 at 9 epochs, 3 at 27 epochs, and 1 at 81 epochs. Each total epoch level includes training already completed. Thus

\begin{aligned} \text{checkpoint cost} &=27\times3+9\times(9-3)+3\times(27-9)+1\times(81-27)\\ &=81+54+54+54\\ &=243 \end{aligned}

configuration-epochs. Training every initial configuration fully costs 27\times81=2{,}187 configuration-epochs. Checkpoint continuation therefore uses one ninth of that compute. These are computational costs, not elapsed times under parallel execution.

Part 2: General resource accounting

With restarting, each rung costs

\begin{aligned} p^{[t]}r^{[t]}&=(p^{[0]}\eta^{-t})(r^{[0]}\eta^t)\\ &=p^{[0]}r^{[0]}. \end{aligned}

Summing over T_{\mathrm{SH}}+1 rungs gives total cost (T_{\mathrm{SH}}+1)p^{[0]}r^{[0]}. For continuation, the initial rung costs p^{[0]}r^{[0]}, while every later rung costs

\begin{aligned} p^{[t]}(r^{[t]}-r^{[t-1]}) &=p^{[0]}\eta^{-t}r^{[0]}(\eta^t-\eta^{t-1})\\ &=p^{[0]}r^{[0]}\left(1-\frac1\eta\right). \end{aligned}

Consequently,

\text{continuation cost}=p^{[0]}r^{[0]}\left[1+T_{\mathrm{SH}}\left(1-\frac1\eta\right)\right].

For the numerical bracket, p^{[0]}r^{[0]}=81 and T_{\mathrm{SH}}=3. Restarting costs 4\times81=324; continuation costs 81[1+3(2/3)]=243, as in Part 1.

At a common nominal restarting budget, brackets can have different numbers of rungs. The ratio of continuation cost to nominal cost is

\frac{1+T_{\mathrm{SH}}(1-1/\eta)}{T_{\mathrm{SH}}+1},

which depends on that number. Ignoring rounding, equal nominal budgets therefore need not yield equal incremental compute. A wide bracket initially tests more configurations at lower resource levels; that breadth increases its exposure to unreliable early rankings.

Part 3: Noise and slow starters

Under independent errors,

\bar D_n\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\Delta+b,\frac{\tau^2}{n}\right).

Standardizing the discard event gives

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(\text{discard A}) &=\mathbb{P}(\bar D_n>0)\\ &=1-\Phi\left(-\frac{(\Delta+b)\sqrt n}{\tau}\right)\\ &=\Phi\left(\frac{(\Delta+b)\sqrt n}{\tau}\right). \end{aligned}

When b=0, the mean difference is -0.02, and the discard probability tends to zero. With b=0.03, the early mean difference is 0.01, and the probability tends to one. More validation dates make the early ranking more precise, but that ranking systematically favors B when A’s slow-starting disadvantage exceeds its full-budget advantage. Increasing validation precision alone cannot correct that reversal.

Part 4: Serially dependent validation losses

There are n variance terms and n-h pairs at lag h, so

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\bar D_n) &=\frac{1}{n^2}\left[n\tau^2+2\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}(n-h)\tau^2\rho^h\right]\\ &=\frac{\tau^2}{n}\left[1+2\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac hn\right)\rho^h\right]. \end{aligned}

Joint Gaussianity and b=0 then imply

\mathbb{P}(\text{discard A}) =\Phi\left( \frac{\Delta\sqrt n}{\tau\sqrt{1+2\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}(1-h/n)\rho^h}} \right).

For n>1 and \rho>0, the variance exceeds \tau^2/n. Since \Delta<0, the argument of \Phi moves toward zero, increasing the discard probability toward one half relative to independence. Averaging over many firms at the same dates does not eliminate persistent common shocks in these date-level loss differences.

A larger initial training budget can reduce the slow-starting disadvantage b by evaluating models later in their learning curves. A longer validation window can reduce sampling noise, although serial dependence reduces its information content and changing economic regimes may limit the relevance of old observations. Hyperband’s savings are most useful when early performance is informative about full-budget performance; neither checkpoint accounting nor precise evaluation of a biased early ranking ensures that condition.
