5 Feed-Forward Neural Networks

5.1 Overview

Feed-forward neural networks are best understood as flexible nonlinear regression models. In classical econometrics, we often begin with a parametric specification such as a linear model, a polynomial expansion, or a spline basis. A feed-forward neural network keeps the same basic goal, estimating a conditional mean or conditional class probability, but replaces curated basis functions by a learned composition of affine transformations and nonlinear activation functions.

For econometricians, the main attraction is flexibility. When the relationship between Y and \mathbf{X} is nonlinear, high-dimensional, or shaped by complicated interactions, a neural network offers a way to approximate that relationship without fully specifying it in advance. This is useful in applications such as the nonlinear asset-pricing setting studied by Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) and in other prediction problems with large information sets:

  • Asset pricing: mapping many firm characteristics into expected returns
  • Macroeconomic forecasting: combining large predictor sets, survey variables, or text-based indicators
  • Household or firm behavior: approximating nonlinear production relationships or treatment-response interactions

5.2 Roadmap

  1. We begin with the simplest building block, a neuron or unit, and then build up feed-forward networks as flexible nonlinear basis expansions.
  2. We then discuss activation functions, the transformations that make successive layers nonlinear, and an approximation theorem that explains what sufficiently wide networks can represent.
  3. Next, we connect neural-network estimation to loss minimization, maximum likelihood, and gradient descent, before introducing backpropagation as an efficient way to reuse chain-rule derivatives across layers.
  4. We then show that the training objective is non-convex, connect the fitted network to familiar econometric objects, and explain why its individual weights are not identified.
  5. We close with applications in asset pricing and macroeconomic forecasting and a practical training workflow.

5.3 Basic Architecture

A neural network is a composition of affine maps and nonlinear activations, organized in layers with weights that adjust during learning. We use the standard econometric convention that Y and \mathbf{X}=(X_1,\ldots,X_p)^\top denote the random outcome and random predictor vector, lowercase y and \mathbf{x} denote their realized values, and a hat denotes a fitted value. The cleanest entry point is a single neuron, also called a unit. For a realized predictor vector \mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_p)^\top, let \mathbf{w}=(w_1,\ldots,w_p)^\top be a vector of adjustable coefficients called weights, and let b be an adjustable intercept called the bias. The unit first forms the affine score z=\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{w}+b and then applies an activation function g to that score. Its scalar prediction for Y at input \mathbf{x} is

\hat y(\mathbf{x}) = g(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{w}+b).

We use unit and neuron interchangeably. Historically, perceptron refers more narrowly to a threshold-based linear classifier; a sigmoid neuron is a smooth generalization rather than the original perceptron model. If g(z)=z is the identity function, the unit defines a linear predictor. It becomes linear regression only after specifying a continuous response model, a regression loss, and an estimation procedure. For binary classification, a common choice is the sigmoid function

\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},

which maps any real-valued score z monotonically into the interval (0,1). We can interpret \hat p=\sigma(z) as a fitted probability and classify an observation as Class 1 when \hat p>0.5. Because \sigma(0)=0.5, this classification rule has decision boundary z=\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{w}+b=0. Under symmetric 0–1 misclassification loss, the 0.5 cutoff is Bayes-optimal when the output equals the conditional class probability \Pr(Y=1\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}). With an asymmetric loss — the normal case when the two errors have different economic costs, as in default or recession prediction — the optimal threshold moves away from 0.5.

With a fixed activation g, the unit is precisely a generalized linear model (GLM)-type index specification. One convention is worth pinning down, because the GLM vocabulary runs in the opposite direction: in a GLM the link maps the conditional mean to the linear index, so the object playing the role of our activation g — which maps the index back to the mean — is the inverse link (also called the response or mean function). The logistic regression a student has already met uses the logit link, and its inverse is exactly the sigmoid \sigma above. If the activation were instead left unknown and estimated, we would have a semiparametric single-index model — a generalization of the GLM family. Composing multiple such neurons is what distinguishes a neural network from the GLM baseline.

Figure 5.1 illustrates this rule for two predictors using \mathbf{w}=(-0.2,1)^\top and b=-1.4. The dashed line collects the predictor combinations for which the score is zero.

In Figure 5.1, every Class 1 point has \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{w}+b>0 and hence a fitted probability above 0.5, while every Class 0 point has \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{w}+b<0 and hence a fitted probability below 0.5. The sigmoid transforms the score nonlinearly, but the decision boundary of this single unit remains a straight line. A feed-forward network simply stacks many such units, so that the outputs of one layer become regressors for the next layer. That is why neural networks can be understood as learned basis expansions rather than as a fundamentally alien model class. In a polynomial or spline regression, the transformed regressors are chosen before estimation; in a neural network, the hidden activations a_j^{(l)} (the activation of unit j in layer l, defined formally below) are themselves learned from the data.

A network is described in part by its depth D. Under the convention used here, it has D-1 hidden layers followed by an output layer, which we index D. The output layer need not contain a single unit. With several output units, the network returns one number per unit. A multi-class classifier — with one output unit for each possible class — is an example developed later in this chapter. Thus the network output is generally a vector, which we denote generically in boldface by \hat{\mathbf y}(\mathbf{x}). Stacking the layer maps gives the composition

\hat{\mathbf y}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a}^{(D)} = f^{(D)} \circ f^{(D-1)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}).

When the network has one output unit, \hat{\mathbf y}(\mathbf{x}) reduces to the scalar \hat y(\mathbf{x}) introduced above. The layers play distinct roles: the input layer holds the realized predictors \mathbf{x}, the hidden layers compute intermediate activations, and the output layer produces the prediction \hat{\mathbf y}(\mathbf{x}). Concretely, each layer l computes:

\mathbf{a}^{(l)} = g^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)} \mathbf{a}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right)

Here:

  • \mathbf{a}^{(l)} is the output of layer l (with \mathbf{a}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x})
  • H^{(l)} is the number of units (the width) of layer l, with H^{(0)}=p the input dimension
  • \mathbf{W}^{(l)} is the weight matrix for layer l, with dimensions H^{(l)} \times H^{(l-1)}. Its entry W_{hj}^{(l)} connects neuron j in layer l-1 to neuron h in layer l
  • \mathbf{b}^{(l)} is the bias vector for layer l
  • g^{(l)} is the activation function for layer l, applied per vector entry
  • \boldsymbol{\theta} represents all hidden-layer and output-layer parameters

Moreover, we define the pre-activation value in layer l: \mathbf{z}^{(l)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}.

Single-Hidden-Layer Networks

Much of this chapter, and all of its exercises, works with single-hidden-layer networks, which have the form:

f(x; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{j=1}^{H} w_j^{(2)} g(w_j^{(1)} x + b_j^{(1)}) + b^{(2)}

where:

  • H is the number of hidden units
  • w_j^{(1)}, b_j^{(1)} are the input-to-hidden weights and biases
  • w_j^{(2)}, b^{(2)} are the hidden-to-output weights and bias
  • g(\cdot) is the activation function

This display is written for a scalar predictor x, which keeps the algebra readable and is all the exercises need. For a predictor vector \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p, nothing changes except that the scalar product w_j^{(1)} x becomes the inner product \mathbf{w}_j^{(1)\top}\mathbf{x} with \mathbf{w}_j^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^p; the parameter count below is stated for that general case.

Why Width and Depth Matter Quantitatively A useful discipline is to count parameters before discussing “big” or “small” networks. If the input dimension is p, a single-hidden-layer network with H hidden units and one scalar output contains

(p+1)H + (H+1) = H(p+2)+1

parameters: pH input-to-hidden weights, H hidden biases, H hidden-to-output weights, and one output bias. Even moderate changes in width can therefore increase estimation variance quickly when p is already large.

More generally, under this chapter’s convention that depth D counts the parameterized layers and excludes the input layer, a fully connected network with hidden widths H^{(1)}, \dots, H^{(D-1)} and a scalar output (so H^{(D)} = 1) has

\sum_{l=1}^{D} \big(H^{(l-1)} + 1\big) H^{(l)}, \qquad H^{(0)} = p, \quad H^{(D)} = 1,

parameters. Each layer contributes its weight matrix plus one bias per unit. (For D = 2 this reduces to the single-hidden-layer count H(p+2)+1 above.)

Question for Reflection

Suppose a macro forecasting problem has p=40 predictors and you compare one hidden layer with H=10 against one hidden layer with H=100. How many parameters does each network have, and what does that imply for overfitting risk?

Suggested Answer

With one scalar output, the count is H(p+2)+1. For H=10, this gives 10(42)+1=421 parameters. For H=100, it gives 100(42)+1=4201 parameters. The larger network is much more flexible, but it also has roughly ten times as many free parameters, so it can fit idiosyncratic sample noise much more easily unless the sample is large and validation supports the extra complexity.

5.4 Activation Functions and Universal Approximation

The activation function controls both the shape a unit can represent and the gradient signal available during estimation. Six choices recur in this and the following network chapters:

  1. The sigmoid, introduced in Section 5.3, is \sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z}). Its range is (0,1) and its derivative is \sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)), which makes it a natural output activation for a binary class probability.

  2. The hyperbolic tangent (tanh) is \tanh(z)=(e^z-e^{-z})/(e^z+e^{-z}). It maps into (-1,1) and satisfies \tanh'(z)=1-\tanh^2(z). Its zero-centered output is useful in the recurrent architectures introduced later, although its derivative also becomes small when the unit saturates — that is, when the pre-activation is far enough from zero that the activation sits in one of its flat regions, here near \pm 1, where the derivative is close to zero.

  3. The rectified linear unit (ReLU) is \text{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z). Its range is [0,\infty) and its derivative is 1 for z>0 and 0 for z<0; at the kink z=0, software must choose a convention, usually 0, as the callout below explains. ReLU is useful in hidden layers, but it is a poor output activation for non-negative regression: once an output pre-activation becomes negative, its zero derivative supplies no signal that can move the unit back into the positive region.

  4. The softplus function, \text{Softplus}(z)=\ln(1+e^z), is a smooth approximation to ReLU with range (0,\infty). Its derivative is strictly positive, which makes it a safer output activation when a continuous prediction must remain positive.

  5. For a K-class outcome, the softmax maps an output-score vector into probabilities:

    \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{q=1}^K e^{z_q}}, \qquad k=1,\ldots,K.

    Every coordinate lies in (0,1) and the coordinates sum to one. Its derivative is

    \frac{\partial \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k}{\partial z_q} =\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k\big(\delta_{kq}-\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})_q\big),

    where \delta_{kq}=1 if k=q and 0 otherwise is the Kronecker delta, distinct from the backpropagation error signal introduced in Section 5.6.

  6. The linear, or identity, activation is \text{Identity}(z)=z. Its unrestricted range makes it the standard output activation for an unbounded continuous regression target.

Each function provides a different kind of nonlinearity, from the bounded “S-shape” of the sigmoid and tanh to the unbounded, one-sided linearity of ReLU and its smooth counterpart, Softplus.

Additional detail: ReLU at the kink

ReLU is not differentiable at z=0. Implementations therefore assign a conventional value to \operatorname{ReLU}'(0), usually 0 (Goodfellow, Bengio, and Courville 2016). We return to what this convention means for network gradients after introducing backpropagation below.

Practical selection. For a feed-forward hidden layer, ReLU is a transparent baseline: away from its kink, positive pre-activations pass their gradient through unchanged instead of multiplying it by a derivative below one. This mitigates vanishing gradients — gradient signals shrinking toward zero through saturated activations — relative to sigmoid or tanh units (Glorot, Bordes, and Bengio 2011). Tanh remains relevant when the architecture specifically benefits from bounded, zero-centered hidden states, as in the recurrent networks of the coming chapters.

For the output layer, the support and interpretation of the prediction determine the activation:

  • Regression problems (unbounded): Linear activation
  • Regression (positive): Softplus
  • Binary classification: Sigmoid for probability interpretation
  • Multi-class Classification: Softmax

Why Activation Functions Matter Without activation functions, a deep neural network collapses into a single linear model. With two layers of weight matrices W_1 and W_2 and zero biases for exposition, the output of a linear-activation network is again a matrix times the input:

\mathbf{W}_2(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{W}_2 \mathbf{W}_1)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{W}_{new}\mathbf{x}.

Question for Reflection

Using the output-layer guidelines above, what activation is natural for a positive regression outcome such as firm sales or stock-market volatility? If the loss is the mean squared error (MSE), what target is the network learning?

Suggested Answer

Using the tools introduced in this chapter, a natural raw-scale choice is a softplus output with mean squared error. The softplus activation respects the non-negative support of the outcome, while MSE targets a conditional mean. The important econometric point is that the activation restricts the support of the prediction, while the loss determines the statistical target. MSE is about conditional-mean prediction, not the full conditional distribution.

Universal Approximation Theorem

Theorem (Hornik 1991, Theorem 2): Suppose g is a nonconstant, bounded, continuous activation function, such as a sigmoid. For any continuous function f on a compact (i.e., bounded and closed) set K \subset \mathbb{R} and any \varepsilon > 0, there exist a positive integer H and parameters \{w_j^{(1)}, b_j^{(1)}, w_j^{(2)}\}_{j=1}^{H} and b^{(2)} such that the function

\hat f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{H} w_j^{(2)} g(w_j^{(1)} x + b_j^{(1)}) + b^{(2)}

satisfies

\sup_{x \in K} |\hat f(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon.

The theorem is an existence result: it says a suitable width H exists, but does not tell us how to pick H or how to find the weights.

The notation here matches the single-hidden-layer representation introduced above. The same result extends from scalar x to vector-valued \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p, with K \subset \mathbb{R}^p compact and w_j^{(1)} x replaced by \mathbf{w}_j^{(1)\top}\mathbf{x}.

Attribution. This result is often called the “Cybenko–Hornik” theorem, but the two papers do not prove the same statement, and the difference is the hypothesis on g. Cybenko (1989) proves the earlier and weaker version: his activations must be continuous and sigmoidal, meaning g(z) \to 1 as z \to +\infty and g(z) \to 0 as z \to -\infty, and his domain is the unit cube. The bounded-nonconstant-continuous form stated above is Hornik (1991).

The activation condition matters. The statement quoted here is the classical bounded-activation theorem, with sigmoids as the leading example. Modern networks often use ReLU, which is unbounded and therefore not covered by this version of the theorem. However, the extension of the theorem that does cover ReLU is Leshno et al. (1993).

Practical Implications

The theorem provides existence, not construction. It says that some width H suffices, but it does not say how to choose H, how to find the weights, or how easy the resulting optimization will be. The width required in practice can be very large, and the underlying non-convex optimization remains hard whether or not the approximation theorem applies.

With a fixed architecture, the network specifies a finite-dimensional model class: the family of candidate regression functions traced out as the parameter vector containing all weights and biases ranges over its admissible values. Universal approximation establishes that rich functions can be approximated; consistency of the estimated network additionally requires the complexity to grow slowly enough relative to the available data (White 1990; Chen 2007).

5.5 Loss Functions and Estimation

We follow the optimization chapter in writing L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) for the sample-average loss over N observations, and \ell(y_i, f(x_i;\boldsymbol{\theta})) for observation i’s contribution, which that chapter writes as \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Mean Squared Error Loss (Regression)

For regression problems, we typically use the mean squared error:

L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - f(x_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}))^2

The factor of \frac{1}{2} is sometimes included for convenience when taking derivatives. For a fixed network architecture, minimizing this criterion is nonlinear least squares: the fitted values depend nonlinearly on \boldsymbol{\theta}, but the estimator is still defined as a minimizer of a sum of squared residuals. We can employ other loss functions as well, to which we return when discussing distributional networks.

Cross-Entropy Loss (Classification)

For binary classification problems, we typically use the cross-entropy (log-likelihood) loss. Such a network has a single output unit, and we write z_i^{(D)} for that unit’s pre-activation value at input x_i — the real-valued score the network produces before the output sigmoid is applied, in the notation of Section 5.3. Keep this separate from f(x_i;\boldsymbol{\theta}), which denotes the network’s output throughout this chapter: in a classification network the output is the fitted probability \hat p_i, not the score. If y_i \in \{0, 1\} are the true class labels and \hat{p}_i=\sigma(z_i^{(D)}) are the predicted probabilities, where \sigma is the sigmoid function defined above, then

L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N [y_i \log(\hat{p}_i) + (1-y_i) \log(1-\hat{p}_i)]

For multi-class classification, the observed class first has to be represented as a vector.

Definition: One-hot Encoding

With K possible classes, a one-hot vector has length K, contains a 1 in the position of the observed class, and contains 0s elsewhere. For example, if the classes are recession, normal growth, and boom, the three encodings are [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0], and [0, 0, 1]. The encoding records class membership without imposing an ordering on the class labels.

Using this representation, the categorical cross-entropy loss with a softmax output is

L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^K y_{ik} \log(\hat{p}_{ik})

where y_{ik} is the one-hot indicator for class k and \hat{p}_{ik} = \frac{\exp(z_{ik}^{(D)})}{\sum_{q=1}^K \exp(z_{iq}^{(D)})} is the corresponding softmax probability. Here z_{ik}^{(D)} is the class-k output-layer pre-activation for observation i. As in the binary case, the softmax is applied to the scores, and the network’s output is the probability vector (\hat p_{i1},\dots,\hat p_{iK}).

Connection to Information Theory

The cross-entropy loss is not just a convenient formula — it has a direct information-theoretic foundation. Recall from the Information Theory chapter that minimizing cross-entropy between the empirical data distribution and the model is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation, which in turn minimizes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the two distributions. In this sense, training a neural network with cross-entropy loss is maximum likelihood estimation of a Bernoulli (or multinomial) model whose success probability is parameterized by the network.

Two conditions from that chapter carry over, and it is worth being precise about them, because the loose version of this statement is false. First, the sample average L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) is a negative log-likelihood only if the y_i are conditionally independent given the predictors, so that the joint likelihood factorizes into the product of the per-observation Bernoulli terms. For dependent data the corresponding object is the prediction-error decomposition, with the conditioning set \mathcal{F}_{t-1} in place of x_i. Second, “closest to the true conditional distribution” is a population, large-N statement.

Question for Reflection

Why is the cross-entropy loss for binary classification a special case of the categorical cross-entropy loss with K = 2?

Suggested Answer

With K = 2, the one-hot outcome vector has two entries, for example (y_i, 1-y_i), and the softmax probability vector is (p_i, 1-p_i). Plugging these into the categorical cross-entropy loss

-\sum_{k=1}^2 y_{ik} \log \hat p_{ik}

gives

-\big[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\big],

which is exactly the binary cross-entropy loss. So the binary case is not a different principle; it is the two-class version of the general categorical formula.

Gradient Descent Optimization

The optimization chapter developed gradient descent in detail. Here we need only its update rule with learning rate \eta:

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)} - \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})

Unlike the quadratic ordinary least squares (OLS) objective, a neural-network loss is generally non-convex. The returned stationary point can therefore depend on the initialization and learning rate. Figure 5.4 provides a visual reminder of that difference before we turn to the network-specific task: computing \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}L efficiently.

Gradient Descent in the Simplest Possible Network Consider a single-hidden-layer network with one neuron, of the following form:

f(x; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = w_2 g(w_1 x + b_1) + b_2

Note that we deviate from indicating the layer by superscripts because in one of the exercises we use the superscript to denote a gradient descent update for this simple network.

Use the mean squared error loss

L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i-f(x_i;\boldsymbol{\theta}))^2.

For a sigmoid hidden activation, g'(z)=g(z)(1-g(z)). The gradients of this loss for N data points (x_i, y_i) are computed using the chain rule:

Let z = w_1 x + b_1, a = g(z), and \hat{y} = w_2 a + b_2.

For observation i with residual r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i a_i

\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i

\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i w_2 g'(z_i) x_i = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i w_2 a_i(1-a_i) x_i

\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_1} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i w_2 a_i(1-a_i)

5.6 Backpropagation

We now examine how the chain rule computes the gradient with respect to all parameters in a wide multilayer network with the objects defined in Section 5.3. Computing the gradient naively would require a separate chain-rule calculation for each weight and bias, which is inefficient. Backpropagation solves this problem by reusing intermediate quantities from the forward pass to compute all gradients in one backward sweep.

The algorithm, popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams (1986), obtains the derivative for every weight and bias in one backward sweep that reuses intermediate quantities from the forward pass. For a weight from unit j in layer l-1 to unit h in layer l, we want

\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_h^{(l)}}.

Because

L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

differentiating the finite sum lets us derive the gradient for one observation and then average the resulting gradients; for example, the weight gradient is

\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}}.

For the one-observation derivation below, fix i and suppress its index: \ell, a_h^{(l)}, and z_h^{(l)} stand for \ell_i, a_{ih}^{(l)}, and z_{ih}^{(l)}, respectively. The layer index l remains explicit; h and j label connected units, while q is used as a summation index over units.

Error Signal Definition For each unit h in layer l, define the error signal

\delta_h^{(l)} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_h^{(l)}}.

This error signal measures how the loss changes when the pre-activation of unit h is perturbed, holding the other pre-activations in the layer fixed. Writing the pre-activation defined in Section 5.3 entry by entry gives

z_h^{(l)} = \sum_{q=1}^{H^{(l-1)}} W_{hq}^{(l)}a_q^{(l-1)} + b_h^{(l)}.

The weight W_{hj}^{(l)} affects z_h^{(l)} but no other pre-activation in layer l. Moreover, the activations a_q^{(l-1)} are computed from the input and the parameters of layers 1,\dots,l-1, so they do not vary with W_{hj}^{(l)}. Treating the vector \mathbf z^{(l)} as an intermediate quantity, the multivariate chain rule therefore gives

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}} = \sum_{k=1}^{H^{(l)}} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_k^{(l)}} \frac{\partial z_k^{(l)}}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}}.

Only the term k=h is nonzero. Differentiating the display for z_h^{(l)} with respect to W_{hj}^{(l)} leaves only the term q=j, so

\frac{\partial z_h^{(l)}}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}} = a_j^{(l-1)}.

Consequently, the weight gradient is

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}} = \delta_h^{(l)}a_j^{(l-1)}.

The same reasoning applies to the bias. Since b_h^{(l)} affects only z_h^{(l)} and

\frac{\partial z_h^{(l)}}{\partial b_h^{(l)}}=1,

the bias gradient is

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b_h^{(l)}} = \delta_h^{(l)}.

Although the loss may depend on z_h^{(l)} through many downstream paths, all those paths are summarized by \delta_h^{(l)}. Once the error signals for layer l are available, the stored activation a_j^{(l-1)} supplies the remaining factor: each weight derivative requires one multiplication, while each bias derivative is obtained directly from the error signal.

Backward Pass

For an element-wise output activation, the output-layer signal is

\delta_h^{(D)} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_h^{(D)}} \frac{\partial a_h^{(D)}}{\partial z_h^{(D)}} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_h^{(D)}} g'^{(D)}(z_h^{(D)}).

This formula assumes that a_h^{(D)} depends only on its own pre-activation. Softmax is the notable exception because every output coordinate depends on every pre-activation due to the normalization constraint. Its signal therefore uses the full Jacobian,

\delta_k^{(D)} = \sum_{q=1}^{K} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_q^{(D)}} \frac{\partial a_q^{(D)}}{\partial z_k^{(D)}}.

With cross-entropy, the sum simplifies to \delta_k^{(D)} = \hat{p}_k-y_k: fitted class probability minus the one-hot indicator. Exercise 5.3 derives the analogous residual form for a sigmoid output and Bernoulli cross-entropy.

How can we reuse this information to calculate \delta_h^{(l)} for hidden layers l = D-1, D-2, \ldots, 1?

Step 1: Apply chain rule through the activation function:

\delta_h^{(l)} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_h^{(l)}} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_h^{(l)}} \frac{\partial a_h^{(l)}}{\partial z_h^{(l)}} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_h^{(l)}} g'^{(l)}(z_h^{(l)}).

Step 2: Activation a_h^{(l)} affects every pre-activation in layer l+1, so the multivariate chain rule gives

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a_h^{(l)}} = \sum_{q=1}^{H^{(l+1)}} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_q^{(l+1)}} \frac{\partial z_q^{(l+1)}}{\partial a_h^{(l)}} = \sum_{q=1}^{H^{(l+1)}} W_{qh}^{(l+1)} \delta_q^{(l+1)}.

Notice that W_{qh}^{(l+1)} connects unit h in layer l to unit q in layer l+1.

Combining Steps 1 and 2 gives the backpropagation recursion. Each backward step consumes \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}, which the preceding backward step already produced, and computes the activation derivative once from the stored pre-activation:

\delta_h^{(l)} = \underbrace{\left(\sum_{q=1}^{H^{(l+1)}} W_{qh}^{(l+1)} \delta_q^{(l+1)}\right)}_{\text{reused from layer } l+1} \underbrace{g'^{(l)}(z_h^{(l)})}_{\text{computed once at layer } l}.

Matrix Notation: The backward pass can also be written in matrix notation, which makes the same decomposition visible at the level of vectors:

\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \underbrace{\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}}_{\text{reused from step } l+1} \odot \underbrace{g'^{(l)}(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})}_{\text{computed fresh at layer } l},

where \odot denotes the element-wise (Hadamard) product. The transpose ensures that gradients “flow backward” through the same connections that data flowed forward through, but in reverse order and with transposed weight matrices.

ReLU kinks and the computed gradient

Suppose the loss and all other activations are differentiable, so ReLU is the network’s only nonsmooth component. If no ReLU pre-activation for any training observation equals zero, the sample objective is differentiable at the current parameter value and the backpropagation recursion returns its ordinary gradient. If some pre-activation does equal zero, ReLU’s derivative does not exist. The composite objective may or may not be differentiable at that parameter value, but standard implementations still apply the chain rule using their assigned value for \operatorname{ReLU}'(0).

Question for Reflection

Suppose you ignored the recursion and instead computed each \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}} separately, applying the chain rule from the loss all the way down to layer l each time. Which quantities would you recompute, and which does the backward pass compute only once?

Suggested Answer

Computing the gradient for a weight in layer l from scratch requires propagating the derivative through every layer between the output and layer l — that is, re-deriving the entire chain \delta^{(D)}, \delta^{(D-1)}, \dots, \delta^{(l)}. Repeating this separately for parameters recomputes substantial portions of the same chain. Under a simplified fixed-width comparison, that repeated work can introduce an additional factor proportional to depth.

The recursion avoids this because \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} is computed once from \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}, and everything above layer l is already summarized in that one vector. The forward pass stored \mathbf{z}^{(l)} and \mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}. At layer l, the backward pass evaluates g'^{(l)} once at the stored \mathbf{z}^{(l)} and performs one matrix-vector product with \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)\top}. The gradients for all weights in the layer then follow from \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} and \mathbf{a}^{(l-1)} without further propagation. Reverse-mode backpropagation therefore costs a small constant multiple of the forward pass. Both passes scale with the network’s active connections and arithmetic operations; the advantage is reuse, not independence from the number of parameters.

Full Gradient Update Step

In your optimization algorithm, you are currently at parameter value \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}. The two passes below run for one observation at a time; the resulting per-observation gradients are then averaged over the mini-batch before the parameter update, exactly as in the optimization chapter.

Part 1: Perform a Forward Pass of Your Data

For an observation with predictors \mathbf x, compute the network at the current parameters. Store the pre-activations and activations for each layer:

  • \mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}
  • \mathbf{z}^{(l)}

for l=1,\dots,D, together with the final output \mathbf{a}^{(D)}. The backward pass will evaluate each activation derivative from the stored \mathbf z^{(l)}.

Part 2: Reverse Iteration of Gradients

  1. Compute the output-layer error signal \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(D)}, and immediately convert it into the output-layer gradients:

    \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_{hj}^{(D)}} = \delta_h^{(D)} a_j^{(D-1)} \text{ and } \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b_h^{(D)}} = \delta_h^{(D)}.

  2. For l = D-1, \dots, 1:

    2.1 Calculate the error signal for layer l by combining the \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)} just produced in the previous iteration with the activation derivative at layer l:

    \delta_h^{(l)} = \underbrace{\left(\sum_{q=1}^{H^{(l+1)}} W_{qh}^{(l+1)} \delta_q^{(l+1)}\right)}_{\text{reused from layer } l+1} \underbrace{g'^{(l)}(z_h^{(l)})}_{\text{computed once at layer } l}.

    2.2 Use \delta_h^{(l)} to compute the gradients with respect to the weights and biases in layer l:

    \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_{hj}^{(l)}} = \delta_h^{(l)} a_j^{(l-1)} \text{ and } \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b_h^{(l)}} = \delta_h^{(l)}.

    2.3 Go back to 2.1 until l = 1

Every layer l = 1, \dots, D is visited exactly once, so the loop returns the gradient with respect to all weights and biases, output layer included.

This reuse of intermediate derivatives is the efficiency gain of backpropagation versus naive differentiation.

5.7 Non-Convexity and Econometric Interpretation

The Hessian matrix contains all second partial derivatives:

\mathcal{H}_{jk} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \theta_j \partial \theta_k}

(We write \mathcal{H} for the Hessian to avoid a clash with the layer width H.)

A twice-differentiable function on an open convex domain is convex if and only if its Hessian is positive semidefinite (PSD) at every point of that domain. Neural-network loss functions are nonlinear in the parameters (because of the activation function g), and the loss is in general not a convex function of \boldsymbol \theta. A clean way to see this is a symmetry argument. For the sigmoid, g(-z) = 1 - g(z), so the parameter vectors

\boldsymbol\theta = (w_1, b_1, w_2, b_2) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boldsymbol\theta_{\text{mir}} = (-w_1, -b_1, -w_2, b_2 + w_2)

implement exactly the same function f(x) = w_2\, g(w_1 x + b_1) + b_2 and therefore attain exactly the same loss, L(\boldsymbol\theta) = L(\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{mir}}).

Now take any \boldsymbol\theta and look at the midpoint \tfrac12\boldsymbol\theta + \tfrac12\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{mir}} = (0, 0, 0, b_2 + w_2/2). Because its hidden-to-output weight is zero, this parameter vector represents a constant function. If L were convex we would have

L\big(\tfrac12\boldsymbol\theta + \tfrac12\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{mir}}\big) \;\le\; \tfrac12 L(\boldsymbol\theta) + \tfrac12 L(\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{mir}}) \;=\; L(\boldsymbol\theta)

for every \boldsymbol\theta — that is, some constant fit would be at least as good as every network in the class. Unless the best attainable fit really is a constant, that is false, so the loss cannot be convex. Note that this version of the argument never assumes a minimizer exists, which matters because on an unbounded parameter space the infimum need not be attained.

The same symmetry also speaks to uniqueness. Whenever a minimizer does exist, it is not unique: if the optimal fit is non-constant then any minimizer has w_2 \neq 0, and its mirror image is a different parameter vector with the same loss; and if the optimal fit is constant, then every (w_1, b_1, 0, c) with the same c attains it, which is a continuum. We return to what this non-uniqueness means for interpreting estimated weights in the discussion of identification below.

Example: Non-convexity in Simple Networks

For the network f(x) = w_2 g(w_1 x + b_1) + b_2, the cross-partial derivative is:

\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial w_1 \partial w_2} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N a_i(1-a_i)x_i \big( w_2 a_i - r_i \big),

with a_i = g(w_1 x_i + b_1). This expression can be positive or negative depending on the data and parameter values. On its own, a sign-varying cross-partial does not disprove convexity — off-diagonal entries of a PSD Hessian can take either sign — but it illustrates how parameters interact across layers, creating the curvature structure that the symmetry argument above exploits. Exercise 5.2 asks you to derive this cross-partial from the first-order gradients, and to say precisely what a sign-varying off-diagonal entry does and does not establish.

Connection to Econometric Concepts

From an econometric perspective it pays to keep four objects apart, because the claims made in the rest of this chapter attach to different ones and are not interchangeable.

The population target is the regression function

m(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}],

an unknown feature of the joint distribution of (Y, \mathbf{X}). It exists whether or not anyone ever fits a network to data.

The model class is the family of candidate functions the architecture can produce,

\mathcal{M}_\Theta = \big\{\, f(\cdot\,; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \;:\; \boldsymbol{\theta} \in \Theta \,\big\},

where \Theta denotes the set of admissible parameter vectors. At an arbitrary \boldsymbol{\theta} the network is a parameterized candidate function and nothing more. Since \boldsymbol{\theta} is a free argument rather than a function of the sample, f(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{\theta}) is not an estimator of anything.

The estimator is defined by the rule that lets the sample choose the parameter value. Write \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} for the value that this rule selects from the minimizers of the criterion below, treating any tie-breaking convention as part of the rule; because it is a function of the sample, it is a random object, and so is the fitted regression function

\widehat m(\mathbf{x}) := f(\mathbf{x}; \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

So \widehat m is an estimator of m in the ordinary sense: a rule mapping a sample to a function, random because the sample is. The one curve it returns on the dataset in front of us is the corresponding estimate. The distinction earns its keep below, because claims about sampling variability are claims about the rule, not about the single curve we happen to have drawn. (We continue to write \hat y and \hat y_i for the network’s output at whatever parameter value is currently held — during optimization, that is not \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} — and reserve \widehat m for the fitted estimator.)

Correct specification means m \in \mathcal{M}_\Theta: the target is exactly representable by some parameter value in the class. It does not mean \widehat m = m. Even under correct specification the two differ in any finite sample, because \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} is computed from noisy data and estimation error remains. Under misspecification, m \notin \mathcal{M}_\Theta, and an approximation error is present on top of the estimation error.

The estimation rule selects a minimizer of the sample loss:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \in \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ell\big(y_i, f(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})\big),

where \ell is the loss appropriate to the prediction problem, such as squared loss or cross-entropy. This is the same sample-average criterion introduced above, now written in terms of the full network f.

Non-identification of \boldsymbol{\theta}. The \arg\min above should be read as a set, not a point. The symmetry argument in this section already shows that a sigmoid unit and its mirror image give the same fitted function and hence the same loss. A second, more general source of multiplicity appears in a layer with H>1 units: permuting the units together with their incoming and outgoing weights leaves the network’s output unchanged. This operation generates up to H! parameter vectors, which are distinct except at parameter values that themselves have symmetries. The map from parameters to functions is therefore many-to-one, and \boldsymbol{\theta} is not identified, even in population and with infinite data.

This has a concrete consequence that matters more in econometrics than in machine learning: an individual estimated weight has no structural interpretation, and a standard error attached to it is not interpretable the way an OLS coefficient’s standard error is. The fitted prediction function is invariant to these unit relabelings, so it is the economically relevant object. Invariance alone does not make the fitted function unique: distinct functions can attain the same minimum sample loss. At the population level, identifying the regression function as a unique risk minimizer additionally requires the usual uniqueness and support conditions. These qualifications are why the chapter evaluates predictions out of sample rather than interpreting individual weights.

One further caution belongs here, because the slide from one to the other is easy to make. Even a network that predicts well out of sample gives \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} no causal interpretation. Good predictive performance is evidence that \widehat m approximates the conditional mean m under the distribution that generated the data; it is not evidence about what would happen to Y if a predictor were intervened on. The non-identification just described sharpens the point: individual weights cannot carry a structural reading in the first place, so there is nothing for a causal interpretation to attach to.

5.8 Applications in Economics and Finance

Asset pricing and portfolio management. Predicting stock returns from a large set of firm characteristics and macroeconomic variables is the flagship application. The traditional route specifies linear factor models with pre-specified factors; feed-forward networks instead capture nonlinear interactions between characteristics and let the data suggest the functional form. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) show that flexible nonlinear specifications can outperform linear benchmarks for individual-stock return prediction.

Macroeconomic forecasting and nowcasting. For predicting gross domestic product (GDP) growth, inflation, or unemployment from high-dimensional datasets, the traditional tools are vector autoregressions (VARs) and dynamic factor models. Feed-forward networks handle many predictors and nonlinear interactions naturally, especially when the problem is cast as one-step-ahead prediction with a large information set. Richardson, van Florenstein Mulder, and Vehbi (2021) nowcast New Zealand GDP growth in real time from roughly 600 domestic and international macroeconomic and financial series, using genuine data vintages so that each nowcast uses only information available at the time. They find that machine-learning algorithms — among them a small feed-forward network with a single sigmoid hidden layer — improve on both an autoregressive benchmark and a dynamic factor model. The real-time design matters econometrically: macroeconomic data are revised, so evaluating on final-vintage data would credit the model with information no forecaster had.

5.9 Practical Training Workflow

The complexity of the optimization problem, together with large modern datasets, explains why practitioners rarely rely on plain batch gradient descent alone.

Batch gradient descent uses the entire training set for each update. Mini-batch gradient descent estimates the gradient from a subset, while stochastic gradient descent uses one randomly sampled observation. Modern training usually uses mini-batches, often with an adaptive optimizer such as Adam, RMSprop, or AdaGrad. The optimization chapter compares these update rules and explains their tuning parameters.

Step-by-step Guide for Coming Up With Good Network Architecture

1. Data Preprocessing

  • Standardize predictors: Neural networks are sensitive to scale. If one predictor is measured in basis points and another in millions of euros, the same parameter step produces very different changes in fitted values across coordinates. Scaling improves the conditioning of the optimization problem, keeps gradient magnitudes comparable, and makes learning-rate choices more stable. Crucially, estimate the scaler’s means and standard deviations on the training split only and apply them unchanged to the validation and test data — estimating them on the full sample leaks distributional information from the evaluation period into training (see the leakage discussion in the cross-validation chapter).
  • Split into training, validation, and test sets
  • For time-series applications, respect the information set available at the forecast date rather than shuffling observations at random

2. Architecture Design

  • Start with one or two hidden layers, which are often enough for tabular econometric data
  • Increase width or depth only if validation performance justifies the extra complexity. Architecture is itself a tuning dimension: Christensen, Siggaard, and Veliyev (2023) compare networks of one to four hidden layers in a realized-variance forecasting exercise, and find that the ranking across depths varies while all of them beat the heterogeneous autoregressive (HAR) benchmark. Their broader finding is a useful corrective in the other direction too — even a shallow network, implemented with minimal tuning, was competitive, so extra depth is not where the gains in that application came from. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) report the sharper version of the same lesson for return prediction: performance there deteriorates beyond roughly three hidden layers
  • Choose an appropriate output activation for the outcome type, using the guidelines in Section 5.4

3. Training Process

  • Use Adam or another mini-batch optimizer as a practical baseline; the optimization chapter explains these update rules and their tuning parameters
  • Monitor both training and validation loss
  • Treat the learning rate and batch size as tuning choices rather than fixed defaults

4. Iteration and Refinement

  • Overfitting: If training loss keeps falling while validation loss begins to rise, simplify the architecture or stop at the best validation iteration
  • Underfitting: If both training and validation losses remain poor relative to a simple benchmark, increase model capacity or continue training

5.10 Summary

Key Takeaways
  1. A feed-forward neural network defines a model class by composing affine transformations and nonlinear activation functions.
  2. The output activation and loss must match the prediction problem: regression, binary classification, or multi-class classification.
  3. Universal approximation is an existence result; it neither chooses an architecture nor guarantees successful estimation.
  4. Backpropagation computes gradients efficiently, but the non-convex objective makes initialization, learning rates, and validation consequential.
  5. Fitting defines an estimator of the prediction function, while individual weights are not identified and have no structural or causal interpretation; judge the fitted function by its out-of-sample performance against simpler benchmarks.
Common Pitfalls
  • Interpreting individual network weights as identified coefficients or causal effects.
  • Assuming that a wider or deeper network must improve prediction instead of validating architecture choices against a simpler benchmark.
  • Letting evaluation-period information enter training, for example by fitting the scaler on the full sample or randomizing a chronological train-validation-test split.

In the construction of Part 2, most of the input-to-hidden weights and biases are pinned down by the knots rather than estimated. Ask which of w_j^{(1)}, b_j^{(1)}, w_j^{(2)}, and b^{(2)} are still free in each of the two cases.

Under the nesting condition in the question, setting the extra kink coefficient to zero reproduces any function from the smaller class. Ask what this implies for the minimized in-sample loss, and whether the same argument says anything about performance on data that were not used for estimation.

Solution

Part 1: Piecewise-Linear Slopes

For each j,

\frac{d}{dx}(x-c_j)_+ = \begin{cases} 0, & x<c_j,\\ 1, & x>c_j. \end{cases}

Hence, on an interval where exactly the first r knots have been crossed, that is, on (c_r,c_{r+1}) for r = 0,1,\dots,K, we have

\frac{d}{dx}f(x) = \alpha_1+\sum_{j=1}^r \gamma_j.

The convention c_0=-\infty makes r=0 the leftmost piece: before the first knot no ReLU term is active, the sum is empty, and the slope on (-\infty,c_1) is simply \alpha_1. The convention c_{K+1}=+\infty makes r=K the rightmost piece, on which all K terms are active. Therefore f is piecewise linear, with slope changes only at the knot locations.

Part 2: Network Representation

Use the identity

x=\mathrm{ReLU}(x)-\mathrm{ReLU}(-x).

Therefore

f(x) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \mathrm{ReLU}(x) - \alpha_1 \mathrm{ReLU}(-x) + \sum_{j=1}^K \gamma_j \mathrm{ReLU}(x-c_j).

This construction is exactly a one-hidden-layer ReLU network with at most K+2 hidden units. One valid choice is:

b^{(2)}=\alpha_0,

two units for the linear term,

(w^{(1)},b^{(1)},w^{(2)})=(1,0,\alpha_1) \qquad \text{and} \qquad (-1,0,-\alpha_1),

and K units for the spline kinks,

w_j^{(1)}=1, \qquad b_j^{(1)}=-c_j, \qquad w_j^{(2)}=\gamma_j, \qquad j=1,\dots,K.

So a one-hidden-layer ReLU network can represent this piecewise-linear spline form exactly.

Part 3: Counting Parameters

Known knots. In the construction of Part 2, all input-to-hidden parameters are pinned down: w_j^{(1)} = \pm 1 and b_j^{(1)} = 0 for the two units representing the linear term, and w_j^{(1)} = 1, b_j^{(1)} = -c_j for the K knot units. Nothing there is estimated. What remains free is the output bias b^{(2)} = \alpha_0 and the K+1 output weights \alpha_1 (which appears with opposite signs on the two linear units) and \gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_K. The count is therefore

\underbrace{1}_{\alpha_0} + \underbrace{1}_{\alpha_1} + \underbrace{K}_{\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_K} = K+2 .

Estimated knots. If the knot locations are also chosen from the data, each c_j frees up the corresponding hidden bias b_j^{(1)} = -c_j, adding K more quantities for a total of 2K+2. Both counts refer, as the exercise specifies, to the spline representation, in which the input weights are fixed at 1 or -1. An unconstrained ReLU network with K+2 hidden units stores more numbers than this, but that parameterization is redundant: positive rescaling of a hidden unit can leave its contribution unchanged, and hidden units can be permuted. The normalized count isolates the effective quantities that determine the fitted function here — the intercept, the linear slope, the kink sizes, and the knot locations.

Part 4: Approximation, Estimation, and Why In-Sample Fit Cannot Arbitrate

The tradeoff. Both counts grow linearly in K. Under the nesting condition in Part 4 — automatic for estimated knots and imposed explicitly for known knots — any f representable with K knots is also representable with K+1 knots: retain the existing knots and set the extra \gamma to zero. The best achievable approximation error is therefore non-increasing in K, and typically strictly decreasing when the true regression function has curvature the current knots cannot follow. On the estimation side, the same N observations must now pin down more free parameters, so each is estimated less precisely and the fitted function inherits more sampling variability. Increasing K therefore trades approximation error against estimation error.

Why in-sample fit cannot arbitrate. Nestedness is also what disqualifies the in-sample criterion. Because the K-knot class is contained in the (K+1)-knot class, the minimized in-sample loss is non-increasing in K by construction, so it can never favor a smaller model, no matter how much of the extra flexibility is spent fitting noise. In-sample fit measures only the first of the two effects. Distinguishing them requires evaluating on data that were not used to estimate the parameters, which is what validation or cross-validation provides.

Solution

Part 1: Gradients and Updates

The loss function is:

L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - f(x_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}))^2

Let z_i = w_1 x_i + b_1, a_i = g(z_i), and \hat{y}_i = f(x_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = w_2 a_i + b_2.

The residual is: r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i

Since L=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_i r_i^2 and \partial r_i/\partial \theta_j = -\partial \hat y_i/\partial \theta_j, every gradient has the form

\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_j} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_i \frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial \theta_j},

so only the four chain-rule factors differ:

\frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial w_2}=a_i, \qquad \frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial b_2}=1, \qquad \frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial w_1}=w_2\,a_i(1-a_i)\,x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial b_1}=w_2\,a_i(1-a_i),

where the last two use \partial a_i/\partial z_i = g'(z_i)=a_i(1-a_i) together with \partial z_i/\partial w_1 = x_i and \partial z_i/\partial b_1 = 1. Substituting these factors gives the gradients.

Gradients:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i a_i

\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i

\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i w_2 a_i(1-a_i) x_i

\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_1} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i w_2 a_i(1-a_i)

The gradient descent updates are

\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\eta \nabla_\theta L(\theta^{(t)}).

Therefore

w_2^{(t+1)} = w_2^{(t)} + \frac{\eta}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i^{(t)} a_i^{(t)}

b_2^{(t+1)} = b_2^{(t)} + \frac{\eta}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i^{(t)}

w_1^{(t+1)} = w_1^{(t)} + \frac{\eta}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i^{(t)} w_2^{(t)} a_i^{(t)}(1-a_i^{(t)}) x_i

b_1^{(t+1)} = b_1^{(t)} + \frac{\eta}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i^{(t)} w_2^{(t)} a_i^{(t)}(1-a_i^{(t)})

The distinguishing activation-derivative factor is the sigmoid derivative

g'(z_i)=a_i(1-a_i),

which enters both hidden-layer gradients and neither output-layer gradient. The sigmoid derivative is absent at the output layer because w_2 and b_2 enter the fitted value linearly once the hidden activation a_i is given.

Part 2: The Cross-Partial Derivative

Start from the expression for \partial L/\partial w_2 derived in Part 1,

\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i a_i ,

and differentiate with respect to w_1. Both factors inside the sum depend on w_1, so the product rule gives

\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial w_1 \partial w_2} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial w_1}\, a_i + r_i \, \frac{\partial a_i}{\partial w_1} \right].

For the two ingredients, with z_i = w_1 x_i + b_1 and a_i = g(z_i),

\frac{\partial a_i}{\partial w_1} = g'(z_i)\, x_i = a_i(1-a_i)\,x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial w_1} = -\frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial w_1} = -w_2\, a_i(1-a_i)\, x_i ,

where the second expression uses \hat y_i = w_2 a_i + b_2. Substituting,

\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial w_1 \partial w_2} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N a_i(1-a_i)\,x_i \big( r_i - w_2 a_i \big) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N a_i(1-a_i)\,x_i \big( w_2 a_i - r_i \big),

which is the expression stated in Section 5.7.

Why sign variation does not prove non-convexity. A twice-differentiable function is convex on an open convex domain if and only if its Hessian \mathcal{H} is positive semidefinite everywhere. Positive semidefiniteness restricts the diagonal entries — they must satisfy \mathcal{H}_{jj} \ge 0 — and it bounds the off-diagonal entries in magnitude, since |\mathcal{H}_{jk}| \le \sqrt{\mathcal{H}_{jj}\mathcal{H}_{kk}}. But it places no restriction on their sign: the matrix

\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}

is positive definite (its determinant is 1 and its leading entry is positive) and has a negative off-diagonal entry. So observing that \partial^2 L/\partial w_1 \partial w_2 takes both signs across the parameter space is entirely compatible with convexity, and proves nothing on its own.

What would establish non-convexity is exhibiting a point \boldsymbol{\theta} and a direction \mathbf{v} with \mathbf{v}^\top \mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathbf{v} < 0, or — avoiding second derivatives altogether — violating the definition of convexity directly, which is what the mirror-symmetry argument in Section 5.7 does: it exhibits two parameter vectors with equal loss whose midpoint has strictly larger loss whenever the best fit is non-constant.

Part 3: Saturation and Gradient Bounds

Since g(z)\in(0,1) for all z, we have

g'(z)=g(z)(1-g(z)).

For u\in(0,1), the quadratic u(1-u) is maximized at u=1/2, where it equals 1/4. Therefore

0<g'(z)\le \frac{1}{4}.

Hence

\left|\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}\right| = \left|-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i w_2 g'(z_i)x_i\right| \le \frac{|w_2|}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N |r_i|\,|g'(z_i)|\,|x_i| \le \frac{|w_2|}{4N}\sum_{i=1}^N |r_i x_i|.

Similarly,

\left|\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_1}\right| \le \frac{|w_2|}{4N}\sum_{i=1}^N |r_i|.

These bounds condition on the current values of w_2, the residuals, and the inputs. Because those quantities are not bounded by the derivative argument, the inequalities are not global, parameter-independent caps; they isolate only the at-most-1/4 attenuation contributed by each sigmoid derivative.

When the hidden unit is saturated, z_i is very positive or very negative, so g(z_i) is close to 1 or 0. Then g'(z_i) is close to 0, which makes the hidden-layer gradients very small. This helps explain slow learning: the optimizer receives only weak gradient information for parameters in saturated units.

Part 4: Why This Is Not OLS

OLS also minimizes squared loss, but its fitted value is linear in the parameter vector. That makes the OLS objective a quadratic form in the parameters, hence convex, with a Hessian \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} that does not depend on where in the parameter space it is evaluated.

Here the loss is still based on squared residuals, but the fitted value

\hat y_i=w_2 g(w_1x_i+b_1)+b_2

is nonlinear in the parameters because of the hidden activation. As a result:

  • the gradients contain products such as w_2 a_i(1-a_i), so parameters from different layers interact,
  • the objective is no longer quadratic in (w_1,b_1,w_2,b_2), and the Hessian varies across the parameter space (Part 2),
  • and the optimization landscape can contain flat regions, saddle points, and local minima.

It is tempting to stop here and conclude that nonlinearity in the parameters is what makes the problem non-convex, but that inference does not go through: nonlinear parameterizations can perfectly well produce convex objectives. The conclusion needs the separate argument given in Section 5.7. Because g(-z) = 1-g(z) for the sigmoid, the parameter vectors (w_1,b_1,w_2,b_2) and (-w_1,-b_1,-w_2,b_2+w_2) fit the same function and so attain the same loss, while their midpoint (0,0,0,b_2+w_2/2) fits a constant. Convexity would force that constant fit to be at least as good as the chosen non-constant fit, contradicting the assumption in Part 4. Hence L is genuinely non-convex under the stated condition.

So neural-network estimation differs from OLS not because the loss function changed — it is squared error in both cases — but because the parameterization of the model creates a symmetric, many-to-one, and demonstrably non-convex optimization problem.

Part 5: Shape Restrictions on the Consumption Function

With a single hidden unit, the fitted function is \hat y = w_2 g(w_1 x + b_1) + b_2 with g the sigmoid. Under the stated condition w_1w_2\neq0, the fitted curve is strictly monotone in x: increasing if w_1 w_2 > 0 and decreasing if w_1 w_2 < 0. Because g(z)\in(0,1), its values are bounded between \min\{b_2,b_2+w_2\} and \max\{b_2,b_2+w_2\}. It is a single S-shape: its slope cannot change sign.

For a consumption function, the economically relevant monotone case is w_1w_2>0. Even in that case, boundedness and the shape of the slope are restrictive. The fitted slope is

\frac{d\hat y}{dx} = w_1w_2\,g'(w_1x+b_1),

whose magnitude tends to zero as x moves far in either direction. Fitted log consumption must therefore flatten at both extremes rather than continuing to grow. The specification cannot represent the workhorse constant-elasticity relationship \alpha+\beta x with fixed \beta\neq0 — the log–log specification that ordinary least squares would fit — in which a one-percent increase in income raises consumption by \beta percent at every income level. More generally, the absolute slope is bell-shaped and reaches its maximum where w_1x+b_1=0. When w_1w_2>0, the signed slope, or fitted income elasticity, rises and then falls; when w_1w_2<0, it falls to its most negative value and then rises toward zero. Thus an increasing fit cannot have elasticity that declines over the entire real line, although its elasticity can decline over an observed income range lying entirely to the right of the peak. If w_1w_2=0, the fitted curve is constant, so monotonicity is weak rather than strict. Adding hidden units relaxes these restrictions: a sum of sigmoids can be non-monotone and can hold a nearly constant slope over a wide range, which is the concrete sense in which width buys flexibility here.

Exercise 5.3: Cross-Entropy, Likelihood, and the Sigmoid Output Layer

Suppose we observe binary outcomes y_i \in \{0,1\} and model the conditional class probability by

p_i = \Pr(y_i=1 \mid x_i) = \sigma(z_i), \qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},

where z_i is the network’s output-layer pre-activation at input x_i (the real-valued score before the sigmoid). Write x_{1:N}=(x_1,\dots,x_N) for the full collection of predictors. Assume throughout that, conditional on x_{1:N}, the outcomes are independent Bernoulli variables with

\Pr(y_i=1\mid x_{1:N})=\Pr(y_i=1\mid x_i)=p_i.

  1. Show that the negative Bernoulli log-likelihood contribution of observation i is \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\left[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right], and explain where the conditional independence assumption is used when you combine these contributions into the sample-average loss L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}).
  2. Show that the derivative of the observation loss with respect to the pre-activation z_i is \frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial z_i}=p_i-y_i, and conclude that \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=\frac{p_i-y_i}{N}.
  3. Now suppose we kept the same sigmoid output layer but replaced cross-entropy by squared error, \ell_i^{\mathrm{MSE}} = \tfrac12 (y_i - p_i)^2 with p_i = \sigma(z_i) as before. Derive \frac{\partial \ell_i^{\mathrm{MSE}}}{\partial z_i}. Then compare the two derivatives for an observation that the model gets badly wrong — y_i = 1 with p_i close to 0 — and use the comparison to explain why the likelihood-based loss is usually preferred for binary classification.

Exam level. The exercise links binary classification in neural networks back to likelihood theory, derives the key gradient used in backpropagation, and then shows by direct computation why the choice of loss changes the optimizer’s behavior. Part 3 reuses the saturation mechanism of Exercise 5.2.

Hint for Part 2
  • Use the chain rule \frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial z_i} = \frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_i}.
  • Remember that \sigma'(z_i)=p_i(1-p_i).
Hint for Part 3
  • The chain rule runs through p_i in exactly the same way as in Part 2; only \partial \ell_i / \partial p_i changes.
  • Once you have both derivatives, evaluate each in the limit p_i \to 0 with y_i = 1. One of them stays bounded away from zero and the other does not.
Solution

Part 1: From Bernoulli Likelihood to Cross-Entropy

By the maintained full conditional specification, conditional on x_{1:N} the marginal distribution of y_i is Bernoulli with success probability p_i, so its probability mass function is p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}. This marginal step uses the assumption that conditioning on the other predictors does not change the success probability; it does not yet use independence across outcomes. Taking logs and negating gives the observation-level negative log-likelihood

\ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\left[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right].

Conditional independence licenses the next step. It lets the joint likelihood conditional on x_{1:N} factorize into a product of the individual Bernoulli terms,

\prod_{i=1}^N p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i},

so that its negative log is the sum \sum_i \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}), and the sample-average loss

L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right]

is exactly 1/N times the negative log-likelihood — and is exactly the binary cross-entropy loss displayed in the chapter. Without the full conditional specification — both the Bernoulli marginals and their factorization — the sum can still be used as a prediction loss or composite criterion, but it is not generally the joint negative log-likelihood. This matters for binary outcomes such as recession indicators or the sign of an asset return, which may be serially dependent; a likelihood can instead be built through the prediction-error decomposition, conditioning each term on the relevant history.

Part 2: Derivative with Respect to the Logit

First,

\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial p_i} = -\frac{y_i}{p_i}+\frac{1-y_i}{1-p_i}.

Since p_i=\sigma(z_i), we also have

\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_i}=p_i(1-p_i).

Hence \begin{align*} \frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial z_i} &= \left(-\frac{y_i}{p_i}+\frac{1-y_i}{1-p_i}\right)p_i(1-p_i) \\ &= -y_i(1-p_i)+(1-y_i)p_i \\ &= p_i-y_i. \end{align*} Since z_i enters L only through \ell_i, it follows that

\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial z_i}=\frac{p_i-y_i}{N}.

Part 3: The Squared-Error Gradient, and Why Cross-Entropy Is Preferred

With \ell_i^{\mathrm{MSE}} = \tfrac12 (y_i - p_i)^2 and p_i = \sigma(z_i), the chain rule gives

\frac{\partial \ell_i^{\mathrm{MSE}}}{\partial z_i} = \frac{\partial \ell_i^{\mathrm{MSE}}}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_i} = -(y_i - p_i)\cdot p_i(1-p_i) = (p_i - y_i)\,p_i(1-p_i).

The two derivatives therefore differ by exactly the factor p_i(1-p_i) = \sigma'(z_i), which the cross-entropy loss cancels and squared error does not.

Now take the badly misclassified case y_i = 1 with p_i \to 0, that is, z_i \to -\infty:

\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial z_i} = p_i - y_i \longrightarrow -1, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell_i^{\mathrm{MSE}}}{\partial z_i} = (p_i-1)\,p_i(1-p_i) \longrightarrow 0 .

Under cross-entropy the gradient stays bounded away from zero, so the optimizer keeps receiving a strong signal precisely for the observations the model gets most wrong. Under squared error the gradient vanishes: the sigmoid is saturated, \sigma'(z_i) is nearly zero, and that factor multiplies the error signal away. The confidently wrong observation produces almost no learning pressure — a slow-learning pathology, and the same saturation mechanism studied in Exercise 5.2, except that here the choice of loss determines whether it bites at the output layer.

To this we can add the interpretive point. Cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of the Bernoulli model (Part 1), so minimizing it is maximum likelihood estimation, with the interpretation of the fitted values as conditional probabilities that comes with it. Squared error is not the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood, but for a binary outcome the unscaled squared error is the Brier score (Brier 1950), a strictly proper scoring rule (Gneiting and Raftery 2007) with its own statistical and decision-theoretic justification; the factor 1/2 used in this exercise is an immaterial positive rescaling. Cross-entropy is especially natural here because it coincides with Bernoulli maximum likelihood and, with a sigmoid output, its gradient does not contain the additional p_i(1-p_i) attenuation factor. It therefore often supplies stronger corrective gradients for confidently wrong predictions.
